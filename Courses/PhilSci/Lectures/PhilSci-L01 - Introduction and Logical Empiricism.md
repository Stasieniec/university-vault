# PhilSci-L01: Introduction and Logical Empiricism

> [!abstract] In short
> What makes a statement meaningful, and what makes a body of statements science?
>
> **The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle:** a statement means something only if it is true by logic alone (like mathematics) or could in principle be checked by observation. Everything else that pretends to describe the world, above all traditional metaphysics, is not false but literally meaningless. Science is the body of statements that pass this test, tied to observation by logic.
>
> The test turned out to be hard to state and too strict for real science, so the movement softened into logical empiricism. Its picture of a scientific theory, a set of sentences linked to observation by rules, was later challenged by the view that a theory is a family of models.
>
> On the mock exam this is Q1 (10 marks): the verifiability criterion of meaning and its significance for demarcation.

## The question

Physics, astrology and theology all make claims about the world. What sets science apart? This is the **problem of demarcation**: where the line between science and non-science runs. The Vienna Circle answered it with a theory of *meaning*. Popper's rival answer is in [[PhilSci-L01b - Popper and Lakatos|L01b]].

A few words the rest of the course uses constantly:

- **Metaphysics:** the study of the nature of things or of being: space and time, causation, free will, how mind relates to matter.
- **Ontology:** the part of metaphysics that asks what things there are.
- **Epistemology:** the study of knowledge: what we can know and how.
- **Semantics:** the study of meaning, including the meaning of formal and mathematical languages.
- **Syntax:** the formal rules for building and transforming expressions in a language, including rules of deduction, with meaning left out.

## The background: experience, reason and Kant

Two old answers to "where does knowledge come from?" set up the whole story.

**Rationalism** (Plato, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz): reason alone can give us real knowledge of the world, independent of the senses. Mathematics is the model: axioms, deduction, certainty. Rationalists seek certainty and necessity, at least in some domains.

**Empiricism** (Aristotle, Locke, Berkeley, Hume, and today van Fraassen): experience is the fundamental source of knowledge about the world. Induction (generalising from observed cases) is central, and empirical knowledge is contingent and fallible: it could have been otherwise, and it could be wrong.

These are positions about knowledge. Positions about what exists are a separate axis: **realism about objects** (things exist independently of minds), **idealism** (objects depend in some way on mind or experience), **realism about ideas** (universals such as "redness" exist independently of minds), **nominalism** (only individual things exist; general terms are just names). The axes cut across each other: Berkeley was an empiricist and an idealist, and a nominalist can be a realist about physical objects.

**Immanuel Kant** (late 18th century) tried to combine the two: empiricism about the *content* of knowledge, rationalism about its *a priori conditions*. Space, time and categories such as causation are not learned from experience; they are the conditions under which we can experience anything at all. His key claim is that some judgements are **synthetic a priori**:

- **Synthetic:** it extends knowledge, rather than unpacking what a concept already contains.
- **A priori:** its justification does not come from particular experiences.

Kant's star example was Euclidean geometry: it tells us about real space, yet we know it without experiment. Hold on to this, because the logical positivists built their position by rejecting it.

## The new tools: modern logic and modern physics

Around 1900 two developments made a new kind of empiricism look possible.

**Logic.** Gottlob Frege built modern first-order logic (variables, the quantifiers $\forall$ "for all" and $\exists$ "there exists", predicates, rules of inference) and founded the philosophy of language. Alfred North Whitehead and Bertrand Russell tried to derive all of mathematics from logic. Ludwig Wittgenstein studied how language relates to reality (his later slogan: "meaning as use"). The lesson the positivists drew: logic is **tautological**, true by form alone and adding no content about the world.

**Physics.** Relativity (1905, 1915) and quantum mechanics (1900 to 1927) overturned what had looked like certain knowledge, and philosophy of science grew out of the need to understand concepts, evidence and theory change in such sciences.

General relativity also undid Kant's example: a non-Euclidean geometry is true of our world, so Euclid was no synthetic a priori truth. The positivists split geometry in two: pure geometry is analytic and only describes possible systems; which system fits our space is an empirical question.

## The Vienna Circle

The **Vienna Circle** (1924 to 1938) was a group of scientifically minded philosophers founded by Moritz Schlick and Otto Neurath, with Rudolf Carnap as its central figure; the logician Kurt Gödel took part. Its early, aggressive phase is called **logical positivism**; the later, moderate phase is **logical empiricism** (some writers use "logical empiricism" for the whole movement).

Their targets were the grand, obscure systems of German philosophy after Kant: G. W. F. Hegel (history as a "world spirit" coming to know itself) and, in their own time, Martin Heidegger (the existentialist philosopher of being and death). Against these they stood for Enlightenment values: reason, clarity, internationalism. Their programme combined empiricism with modern logic: legitimate knowledge is either **empirically grounded** or **logically or mathematically necessary**, and philosophy's job is to clarify the language of science, never to compete with it.

The Circle was destroyed by fascism (Schlick was murdered in 1936, Austria annexed in 1938), and most members fled to the United States, where the movement softened.

## Meaningful statements: analytic or verifiable

The positivists' theory of language rests on two ideas.

**First, the analytic-synthetic distinction.** An **analytic** statement is true or false by its meaning alone: "all bachelors are unmarried". A **synthetic** statement is true or false because of its meaning *and* how the world is: "all bachelors are bald". Analytic truths are necessary, but only because they are empty: they say nothing about the world.

The positivists claimed that all of logic and mathematics is analytic. That let them be thorough empiricists and still explain mathematical knowledge: mathematics does not describe the world, it records how we have decided to use symbols. So, against Kant, there is no synthetic a priori. Whatever we know independently of experience is analytic and empty of factual content.

**Second, the verifiability criterion of meaning.** It applies to every statement that is not analytic, every statement that claims to say something about the world. The idea: to understand such a statement is to know what observation would show it true or false. If no possible observation could bear on it, it does not say anything.

Three clarifications keep this from being misread:

- **In principle, not in practice.** "There are mountains on the far side of the moon" was meaningful before anyone could fly there: we knew what observation would settle it.
- **Not conclusive proof.** There just had to be possible observations that would count for or against the statement. Godfrey-Smith notes that "testability" would have been the better word.
- **Observation in a broad sense:** any kind of sensory experience.

> [!definition] The verifiability criterion of meaning
> A statement is meaningful (scientific) if it is **verifiable**: its truth can in principle be determined through observation or experiment.
>
> "**The meaning of a statement is its method of verification.**"
>
> Together with analytic statements, these are the only meaningful statements. Anything else is a **pseudo-statement**.

The criterion was a weapon. Science and most everyday talk pass it. Poetry and expressions of emotion fail it harmlessly, because they never claimed to state facts. What fails it harmfully is language that claims to state facts and does not: for the positivists, most traditional philosophy, much of ethics, and theology.

## How a word gets its meaning: reduction to observation

Carnap's version starts from words. To fix what a word means, take the simplest sentence it can appear in, its **elementary sentence**: for "stone", that is "$x$ is a stone". Then answer four questions about that sentence $S$:

1. What sentences is $S$ deducible from, and what is deducible from $S$?
2. Under what conditions is $S$ true, and under what conditions false?
3. How is $S$ to be verified?
4. What is the meaning of $S$?

For Carnap these are four formulations of one question. Since for him a theory is ultimately a structure of sentences resting on **observation sentences** (also called protocol sentences, reports of what is observed), knowing a sentence's logical links to observation sentences *is* knowing its truth conditions, its method of verification and its meaning. A word is meaningful when the sentences it can occur in are reducible to observation sentences.

> [!example] Arthropods
> "$x$ is an arthropod" is deducible from, and implies, the sentences "$x$ is an animal", "$x$ has a segmented body", "$x$ has jointed legs". Each of these can be checked by looking. That fixes the meaning of "arthropod": a technical word reduced to observable conditions.

Carnap's made-up word shows the other side. Someone says some things are **"teavy"** and others are not, but there are no empirical signs of teavyness; which things are teavy is an eternal secret. Then "teavy" means nothing. Its user may associate images and feelings with it, but that is a fact about his psychology, and associations do not give a word meaning.

A critical question: are deduction, truth, verification and meaning really one concept? Deduction is about logical consequence, truth about how the world is, verification about evidence, meaning about what an expression says. Equating them needs strong assumptions, and the programme strained when those were questioned.

## The weapon at work: metaphysics as pseudo-statements

The showcase is Carnap's essay "The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932). Earlier critics had called metaphysics false, uncertain or pointless. Carnap's charge is stronger: **meaningless in the strictest sense**. A sterile question ("what is the average weight of Viennese whose phone number ends in 3?") or a false or even self-contradictory statement still has meaning; only meaningful sentences can be true, false, fruitful or sterile. A **pseudo-statement** is a string of words that looks like a statement but does not make one at all.

Pseudo-statements arise in two ways.

**Words with no criterion of application.** "God" has a clear meaning in mythology (beings on Olympus, which could in principle be observed). In metaphysics it is deliberately stripped of any reference to experience and given no new meaning. The same goes for "the Absolute", "the Unconditioned", "the thing in itself", "absolute spirit": they are in the same boat as "teavy".

**Meaningful words combined in a way logical syntax does not allow.** Carnap calls this a **type confusion**: grammar lets you form the sentence, but it cannot be translated into a logically correct form. A typical case is applying a predicate meant for things to "being" or "existence" itself. His target sentence comes from Heidegger's 1929 lecture *What Is Metaphysics?*:

> [!example] "Das Nichts selbst nichtet": "The Nothing itself noths"
> The sentence treats "nothing" first as the name of a thing (the Nothing) and then as a verb (it noths). In modern logic "nothing" is neither. "Nothing is $P$" means
> $$\neg \exists x\, P(x)$$
> "it is not the case that there is an $x$ that is $P$". No object called Nothing appears, so there is nothing that could do any nothing-ing. The sentence is grammatical, but it expresses no proposition. It is not false; it says nothing.

Likewise "something is $P$" is $\exists x\, P(x)$: existence is expressed by the quantifier, and is not an activity a thing performs. So Descartes's "I think" means "there is something that thinks", $\exists x\, P(x)$, and no metaphysical "I" or ego is needed.

If metaphysics says nothing, why have so many brilliant people written it? Carnap's answer: it does have content, just not theoretical content. It **expresses an attitude towards life**. That is a legitimate thing to do, but art does it better: metaphysicians, in his famous line, are "musicians without musical ability". Their mistake is to dress an attitude up as a theory, with arguments and refutations, as if truth were at stake.

### What this means for demarcation

Put the pieces together. Every meaningful statement is either analytic or verifiable. Science consists of verifiable statements (plus the logic and mathematics it uses). So the line between science and non-science becomes a line between meaningful and meaningless factual claims.

> [!definition] Significance for demarcation
> The criterion solves the problem of demarcation: theories whose statements are not verifiable consist of **pseudo-statements** and are **pseudo-science**. They seem to say something (like metaphysics), but they are **literally meaningless**.

So pseudo-science and metaphysics are excluded without anyone having to show them false: they never get as far as being true or false.

Popper, who knew the Circle but was never a member, drew the line differently. His falsifiability criterion separates science from non-science and says nothing about meaning: for him metaphysics can be meaningful, important, and the seed of later science. The full contrast is in [[PhilSci-L01b - Popper and Lakatos|L01b]], under "Not science does not mean nonsense".

## What went wrong with verificationism

**It was hard to state.** The criterion had to be strict enough to exclude metaphysics and liberal enough to admit all of science. Simple versions failed both ways. If "metals expand when heated" is testable, so is "metals expand when heated and the Absolute Spirit is perfect": a metal that does not expand refutes the whole conjunction (if $A$ is false, $A \wedge B$ is false). Metaphysics rides in on the back of science, and each patch opened a new hole. In the other direction, read strictly, the criterion would exclude universal laws, which no finite number of observations can conclusively verify; hence it asked only for possible evidence for or against.

**It seems to undermine itself.** A standard objection, from outside the course readings: the criterion itself is neither analytic nor verifiable by observation, so by its own standard it looks meaningless. (A common defence treats it as a proposal about how to use "meaningful", rather than a factual claim.)

**Theoretical terms resisted reduction.** Science is full of words like "electron" or "gene" whose sentences cannot be translated into observation sentences one by one. The first hope, translating all factual talk into talk about sensations, was soon given up as too extreme.

**Testing is holistic.** W. V. O. Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism", argued that no single statement can be tested on its own. A prediction follows only from a hypothesis plus assumptions about instruments and conditions, so a failed prediction says something in the bundle is false, without saying what. This is the **Duhem-Quine thesis**, covered in [[PhilSci-L03 - Under-determination|L03]]. If statements cannot be tested one at a time, and meaning is method of testing, they do not have meanings one at a time either. The positivists had officially accepted holism about testing, but their criterion still attached observations to each sentence separately.

**The analytic-synthetic distinction itself.** Quine also argued that no sharp line between analytic and synthetic statements can be drawn: in his "web of belief", even statements that look analytic could be revised in response to experience. That attacked the other pillar of the theory of language.

From outside the movement, Popper replaced verifiability with falsifiability ([[PhilSci-L01b - Popper and Lakatos|L01b]]), and Kuhn's history of science ([[PhilSci-L02 - Kuhn on Scientific Practice|L02]]) challenged the idea that theory change could be captured by logic at all.

## The softening: logical empiricism

Carnap was an unusually open-minded thinker, and he changed his view. In the 1930s, after meeting the logicians Gödel and Alfred Tarski (who showed how to define truth precisely for formal languages, the start of formal semantics), he:

- **dropped strict verificationism:** meaning is not just verification;
- **accepted a full semantics:** theoretical terms receive an interpretation of their own, instead of being mere shorthand for observations;
- **weakened the correspondence rules** (the rules linking theoretical terms to observation, see the next section) to allow **partial interpretation**: a theoretical term gets part of its meaning from observation and the rest from its place in the theory. This is much closer to a realist view of science.

He **kept his rejection of metaphysics**, but it lost force: once theoretical terms are interpreted as standing for electrons and fields, he **cannot avoid ontology**, the question of what exists. As Richardson puts it (summarising Thomas Ricketts), "the move to semantics blunts the force of Carnap's diagnosis of metaphysics as confusion." From 1948 Quine went on to revive analytic metaphysics outright.

In 1966 Carnap called his early publications "filled with prohibitory statements", a reaction to German idealism that belonged to its historical situation. He never gave up the demand that philosophy be logical, rigorous and close to science.

In logical empiricism generally (Godfrey-Smith's summary), verification gave way to a **holistic theory of meaning**: a theory is a structure connected to observation as a whole, and a theoretical term gets its meaning from its place in that structure. The analytic-synthetic distinction was kept but treated as questionable. Whether theories describe only patterns in what we observe, or hidden structure behind it, became the question of scientific realism ([[PhilSci-L05 - Scientific Realism and its Critiques|L05]]).

The Carnap-Heidegger clash also marks the split between **analytic** philosophy (logic first) and **continental** philosophy (fundamental philosophical insight first). Friedman traces both to rival schools of Kant's followers and calls this dispute "the fundamental cleavage in 20th-century philosophy".

## What is a scientific theory? Sentences

The logical empiricists' answer is the **syntactic conception**, also called the **Received View** (Putnam's label). A theory is a collection of sentences in a formal logical language, made of three parts:

1. **Axioms:** the theoretical starting points, read as basic laws.
2. **Derived sentences:** everything deduced from the axioms by the rules of the language.
3. **Correspondence rules:** rules linking theoretical terms to observational terms.

It is called syntactic because it treats a theory as a formal system: a vocabulary plus rules for building and deducing sentences.

The key distinction is between **observational terms** ("red", "pointer reading") and **theoretical terms** ("atom", "electron"). Theoretical terms are not directly observable, but they are meaningful because the correspondence rules connect them to the empirical world. An observation sentence contains only observational terms.

```text
axioms (basic theoretical laws)
        |  deduction
        v
derived theoretical sentences
        |  correspondence rules
        v
observation sentences  <-- checked by observation
```

Herbert Feigl pictured it as a network of theoretical concepts floating above the "soil" of observation, meaningful only in so far as empirical concepts anchor it there.

This was a **normative ideal**: a rigorous standard for evaluating scientific knowledge and progress, never meant to describe what goes on in a laboratory.

> [!example] Kinetic theory and the ideal gas law
> **Axioms.** Gases are systems of molecules in motion. Energy and momentum are conserved. Molecules are elastic and obey Newtonian mechanics.
>
> **Correspondence rules.** Pressure $P$ is the mean force with which molecules hit the walls of the container. Absolute temperature $T$ is proportional to the mean kinetic energy of the molecules:
> $$kT = \frac{2}{3}\left\langle \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 \right\rangle$$
> where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $m$ the mass of a molecule, $v$ its speed, and $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the average over all molecules.
>
> **Derivation.** From the axioms alone, kinetic theory gives
> $$PV = \frac{2}{3} N \left\langle \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 \right\rangle$$
> with $V$ the volume and $N$ the number of molecules. Substituting the temperature rule turns the bracket into $\tfrac{3}{2}kT$ and gives the empirical **Boyle-Charles ideal gas law**:
> $$PV = NkT$$
>
> Unobservable molecules, linked by rules to observable pressure, volume and temperature, yield a law you can check in a lab.

## What is a scientific theory? Models

The **semantic conception** starts from three objections to the syntactic one:

1. **Axiomatisation.** Many scientific theories have never been axiomatised, and perhaps never will be.
2. **Language dependence.** One theory can be written in several equivalent ways. Identifying the theory with one of those formulations seems wrong, just as a novel is not identical to one of its translations.
3. **Models.** Scientists actually work with models, and models often carry a theory's content better than a list of sentences.

Its slogan: **a scientific theory is a collection of models**. A model here is a structure defined in mathematics or set theory (a vector space is an example of such a structure) that represents a possible system.

Two senses of "model" need to be kept apart:

- **Iconic or representational model:** a concrete representation that captures features of its target, such as a mechanical model of the solar system. It is structurally similar, ideally **isomorphic**, to what it models (there is a one-to-one mapping between parts and relations that preserves structure).
- **Mathematical or logical model:** a structure that makes the sentences of a theory true. This is the main sense the semantic conception uses.

> [!example] Kinetic theory as models
> A gas of $N$ molecules in a region $C \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3$ of volume $V$ is modelled by points in **phase space**:
> $$\Gamma_N = C^N \times \mathbb{R}^{3N} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^{6N}$$
> $C^N$ holds the positions of the $N$ molecules, $\mathbb{R}^{3N}$ their momenta: three of each per molecule, $6N$ coordinates in all. Laws of motion and collision say how the point moves. Statistical mechanics links these micro-models to thermodynamics: $V$ is fixed by the container, $P$ comes from average momentum transfer to the walls, $T$ from the equilibrium distribution of molecular energies. Under the idealisations, the models satisfy $PV = NkT$. The theory *is* this class of structures.

| | **Syntactic** | **Semantic** |
|---|---|---|
| A theory is | Set of sentences | Class of models |
| Emphasis | Axioms, derivation | How structures apply to systems |
| Link to the world | Correspondence rules | Model fits target system |
| Typical tool | Formal logic | Set theory, mathematics |

### Does the difference survive?

Hans Halvorson argued that "the semantic view, if plausible, is syntactic" (2012, 2013), with replies from Glymour (2013) and van Fraassen (2014). Lutz (2017) concludes the debate does "not capture any significant differences". Frigg (2022): the syntactic view is too strict if it allows only formal sentences, since science needs models and natural language, and the semantic view still needs a language to specify its structures. So the two collapse into each other, or describe the same practice from two sides.

## Where the course goes from here

In the first half of the 20th century, philosophy of science pursued a **normative ideal**: how scientific knowledge can be justified. In the second half it asked **how science actually works**. Kuhn's critique of the received view, that it ignores the historical dynamics of theories, opens that turn ([[PhilSci-L02 - Kuhn on Scientific Practice|L02]]).

>
> A half-page answer: The verifiability criterion of meaning, the central principle of the Vienna Circle and early Carnap, says that a statement about the world is meaningful only if it is verifiable: its truth can in principle be determined through observation or experiment. In the slogan, the meaning of a statement is its method of verification. The only other meaningful statements are analytic ones, those of logic and mathematics, which are true by their form and say nothing about the world. For Carnap a word is meaningful if the sentences it occurs in reduce to observation sentences: "$x$ is an arthropod" is fixed by "$x$ is an animal, $x$ has a segmented body, $x$ has jointed legs". Its significance for demarcation is that it turns the line between science and non-science into a line between meaningful and meaningless. Statements that are neither verifiable nor analytic are pseudo-statements: grammatical, and seeming to say something, but literally meaningless. Metaphysics is the main target: Heidegger's "The Nothing itself noths" treats "nothing" as a name and a verb, whereas logically "nothing is $P$" is just $\neg\exists x\,P(x)$, so the sentence expresses no proposition. Pseudo-science and metaphysics are thus excluded as meaningless rather than false. Popper, by contrast, demarcates science by falsifiability and allows non-science to be meaningful.

>
> - Source: Heidegger's 1929 lecture *What Is Metaphysics?*; Carnap's attack is in "The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932).
> - The sentence treats "nothing" as a **name** of a thing and then as a **verb**. In modern logic it is neither. "Nothing is $P$" is $\neg\exists x\,P(x)$: it is not the case that some $x$ is $P$. There is no object called Nothing that could do anything.

> [!exam]- How was the early logical positivist programme challenged, and how did later logical empiricism differ from it?
> **Key points:** Verifiability hard to formulate (too strict or too liberal); self-undermining objection; theoretical terms not reducible; Quine's holism and attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction; Carnap's move to semantics and partial interpretation, holistic meaning, rejection of metaphysics kept but weakened.
>
> - **Formulation:** the criterion had to exclude metaphysics and include all of science. "Metals expand when heated and the Absolute Spirit is perfect" is testable whenever its first half is; universal laws cannot be conclusively verified.
> - **Self-undermining** (standard objection from outside the readings): the criterion is neither analytic nor verifiable.
> - **Theoretical terms** like "electron" could not be translated into observation sentences.
> - **Quine:** statements face experience only as a whole (Duhem-Quine), so they cannot be tested or given meaning one at a time; and there is no sharp analytic-synthetic line.
> - **External:** Popper's falsifiability, Kuhn's history.
> - **Later logical empiricism:** Carnap, after Gödel and Tarski, dropped strict verificationism, accepted a full semantics and partial interpretation of theoretical terms (closer to realism); meaning became holistic, a term's meaning coming from its place in the theory. > [!exam]- Compare the syntactic and semantic conceptions of theories. Why was the semantic view proposed, and does the distinction survive?
> **Key points:** Syntactic: sentences plus correspondence rules; semantic: class of mathematical models; motivations: no axiomatization, multiple formulations, models in practice; Halvorson, Lutz, Frigg: views collapse or are complementary.
>
> - **Syntactic:** theory = set of sentences (axioms, derivations, correspondence rules), tied to observation by a vocabulary distinction.
> - **Semantic:** theory = **a class of mathematical models** representing possible systems; the emphasis is on how the structures apply to physical systems.
> - Motivations for the semantic view: (1) many theories are **not axiomatized**; (2) one theory has **several equivalent formulations**, so identifying it with one is wrong; (3) scientists actually work with **models**.
> - Does it survive? **Halvorson** (2012, 2013) argues the semantic view, if plausible, is syntactic; replies from **Glymour** (2013) and **van Fraassen** (2014). **Lutz** (2017): the debate does not capture any significant differences. **Frigg** (2022): syntactic is too strict (science needs models and natural language), semantic also needs a language to specify structures. So they collapse into each other or are complementary.

## Flashcards

> [!card]- What is the difference between syntax and semantics?
> Syntax: formal rules for building and transforming expressions, **leaving meaning out**. Semantics: the study of **meaning**.

> [!card]- What does empiricism hold is the fundamental source of substantive knowledge?
> **Experience** and sense perception.

> [!card]- What is the difference between the knowledge rationalism seeks and the knowledge empiricism settles for?
> Rationalism seeks **certainty and necessity**, in at least some domains; empiricism takes knowledge to be **contingent and fallible**.

> [!card]- Why must epistemological and metaphysical positions be kept apart?
> They cut across each other: empiricism is **compatible with idealism**, nominalism with **realism about physical objects**.

> [!card]- How did Kant combine rationalism and empiricism?
> Empiricism about the **content** of knowledge, rationalism about its **a priori conditions**.

> [!card]- For Kant, when is a judgment synthetic?
> When it **extends knowledge** instead of unpacking what a concept already contains.

> [!card]- For Kant, when is a judgment a priori?
> When its justification **does not derive from particular experiences**.

> [!card]- Why did the logical positivists deny that there is synthetic a priori knowledge?
> Everything knowable a priori, including logic and mathematics, is **analytic** and empty of factual content.

> [!card]- How did relativity count against Kant's view of geometry, for the logical positivists?
> A **non-Euclidean** geometry is true of our world, so Euclid was not synthetic a priori.

> [!card]- What status did the logical empiricists give to logic?
> **Tautological** or analytic: true by form, adding no factual content about the world.

> [!card]- What was the Vienna Circle's ideal of legitimate knowledge?
> Knowledge is either **empirically grounded** or **logically/mathematically necessary**.

> [!card]- What is the difference between logical positivism and logical empiricism?
> Logical positivism: the early, aggressive phase. Logical empiricism: the later, **moderate** phase.

> [!card]- What is the difference between an analytic and a synthetic statement?
> Analytic: true or false by **meaning alone**. Synthetic: by meaning **and how the world is**.

> [!card]- Which two kinds of statement are meaningful for early Carnap?
> **Analytic** statements (logic, mathematics) and **empirically verifiable** statements.

> [!card]- What does the verifiability criterion of meaning say?
> A statement is meaningful only if its truth can **in principle** be determined by **observation or experiment**.

> [!card]- Which principle is summed up as "the meaning of a statement is its method of verification"?
> The **verifiability criterion of meaning** (verificationism).

> [!card]- Did verificationism require that a statement can be conclusively proved by observation?
> No: verifiable **in principle**, with possible evidence counting **for or against** it.

> [!card]- How does the verifiability criterion solve the demarcation problem?
> Unverifiable statements are **pseudo-statements**, so theories built from them are **pseudo-science**.

> [!card]- Does verificationism call metaphysical statements false or meaningless?
> **Literally meaningless**: they seem to say something but make no statement at all.

> [!card]- Why is "meaningless" a stronger charge than "false" for Carnap?
> Only **meaningful** sentences can be true or false; a pseudo-statement never gets that far.

> [!card]- What is the elementary sentence of a word, for Carnap?
> The **simplest sentence form** the word can occur in, e.g. "x is a stone".

> [!card]- In Carnap's arthropod example, what does "x is an arthropod" reduce to?
> **x is an animal, x has a segmented body, x has jointed legs**: observation sentences.

> [!card]- When is a word meaningful, for early Carnap?
> When the sentences it can occur in are **reducible to observation sentences**.

> [!card]- What does Carnap's made-up word "teavy" show?
> A word with **no empirical criterion of application** is meaningless, whatever images its user associates with it.

> [!card]- What is the problem with Carnap equating a sentence's entailments, truth conditions, verification and meaning?
> **Deduction, truth, verification and meaning** are not obviously the same; equating them needs strong assumptions.

> [!card]- What are the two ways a pseudo-statement arises, for Carnap?
> Words with **no criterion of application**, or meaningful words combined against **logical syntax**.

> [!card]- What is a "type confusion" for Carnap?
> A grammatical expression that **cannot be translated into logically correct form**, e.g. predicates applied to "being".

> [!card]- Why is Heidegger's "The Nothing itself noths" a pseudo-statement for Carnap?
> It treats "nothing" as a **name** and as a **verb**; in modern logic it is neither.

> [!card]- How does modern logic express "nothing is P"?
> $\neg\exists x\,P(x)$: no object called Nothing is posited.

> [!card]- How does Carnap analyse Descartes's "I think"?
> As "there is something that thinks", $\exists x\,P(x)$, so **no metaphysical ego** is needed.

> [!card]- What does metaphysics express, according to Carnap?
> An **attitude towards life**, which art expresses better: metaphysicians are "musicians without musical ability".

> [!card]- Why does "metals expand when heated and the Absolute Spirit is perfect" trouble verificationism?
> It is **testable** through its first half, so metaphysics slips past the criterion.

> [!card]- Why is the verifiability criterion said to undermine itself?
> It is **neither analytic nor verifiable**, so by its own standard it looks meaningless.

> [!card]- Which two ideas of logical positivism did Quine attack?
> Testing statements **one at a time** (holism instead), and a sharp **analytic-synthetic distinction**.

> [!card]- Why does holism about testing threaten the verifiability theory of meaning?
> If statements are tested only as a whole, they cannot each have **their own** method of verification.

> [!card]- What did Carnap give up after meeting Gödel and Tarski in the 1930s?
> **Strict verificationism**: he accepted a full semantics for theoretical terms.

> [!card]- What did Carnap's partial interpretation of theoretical terms replace?
> The demand for **complete reduction to observations**, moving him closer to a **realist** view of science.

> [!card]- Why did Carnap's rejection of metaphysics lose force after his move to semantics?
> A full semantics **cannot avoid ontology**, so the line between theory and metaphysics blurs.

> [!card]- In later logical empiricism, where does a theoretical term get its meaning?
> From its **place in the whole theoretical structure**, connected to observation as a whole.

> [!card]- What is a scientific theory on the syntactic conception?
> A **collection of sentences in a formal logical language**.

> [!card]- What are the three components of a theory on the syntactic conception?
> **Axioms**, **derived sentences**, **correspondence rules**.

> [!card]- How do theoretical terms like "electron" get meaning on the syntactic conception?
> Through **correspondence rules** tying them to observational vocabulary.

> [!card]- What is the syntactic conception of theories also called?
> The **Received View** of theories.

> [!card]- Is the syntactic conception a description of laboratory practice?
> No, a **normative ideal**: a rigorous standard for evaluating scientific knowledge.

> [!card]- In kinetic theory, what is the correspondence rule for pressure?
> Pressure is the **mean force** with which molecules strike the container walls.

> [!card]- In kinetic theory, what is the correspondence rule for temperature?
> Absolute temperature is proportional to the **mean molecular kinetic energy**.

> [!card]- How does kinetic theory reach the ideal gas law $PV = NkT$?
> Derive $PV$ from the axioms in terms of mean kinetic energy, then **substitute the temperature correspondence rule**.

> [!card]- What is a scientific theory on the semantic conception?
> **A class of mathematical models** representing possible systems.

> [!card]- What three criticisms motivate the semantic conception?
> Many theories are **not axiomatized**; one theory has **several equivalent formulations**; scientists work with **models**.

> [!card]- Why does language dependence count against the syntactic conception?
> One theory has several equivalent formulations, so identifying it with **one formulation** is wrong.

> [!card]- What is the difference between an iconic and a mathematical model?
> Iconic: a **concrete representation** similar to its target. Mathematical: a **structure that makes a theory's sentences true**.

> [!card]- Which sense of "model" does the semantic conception mainly use?
> The **mathematical or logical** sense.

> [!card]- How does the semantic conception represent the microstate of a gas of N molecules?
> As a point in **phase space** with $6N$ coordinates: three position and three momentum per molecule.

> [!card]- What does Halvorson argue about the semantic view?
> The semantic view, **if plausible, is syntactic**.

> [!card]- Why does Frigg think neither conception of theories wins outright?
> Syntactic is **too strict** (science needs models, natural language); semantic **also needs a language** to specify structures.

> [!card]- Does the syntactic/semantic distinction survive, on the recent debate?
> Arguably not: the two **collapse into each other** or are complementary; Lutz finds no significant difference.

> [!card]- How did philosophy of science's focus shift from the first to the second half of the 20th century?
> From the **justification** of scientific knowledge (a normative ideal) to **how science actually works**.
