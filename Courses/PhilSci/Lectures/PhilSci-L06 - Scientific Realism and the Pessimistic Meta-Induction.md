# PhilSci-L06: Scientific Realism and the Pessimistic Meta-Induction

> [!abstract] In short
> Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction says: the aether, phlogiston and caloric do not exist, yet the theories built on them were successful. So success is no sign of truth, and our best theories are probably false too. Can a realist still believe what science says about things we cannot observe?
>
> **Selective realists (Kitcher, Psillos):** yes, if you believe only the parts of a theory that produced its success. The aether did no real work in Maxwell's theory, so losing it costs nothing. **Stanford:** those parts can only be picked out with hindsight, against what the scientists themselves thought, so the win is a Pyrrhic victory.
>
> **De Haro's extensional realism:** keep everything the scientists said, and separate two kinds of meaning: what a word describes (its intension) and what it picks out in the world (its extension). Theories can change their descriptions completely and still pick out the same things where they were tested. So "aether" referred all along, to the electromagnetic field.
>
> No mock exam question is on this lecture (the mock's realism question is in [[PhilSci-L05 - Scientific Realism and its Critiques|L05]]). The last part is the research of Sebastian De Haro, who coordinates the course and sets the exam. The exam questions below are practice questions.

## Where the debate stood after week 5

**Scientific realism** says our best theories are approximately true, so the unobservable things they posit (electrons, fields) really exist. Here is how the arguments of [[PhilSci-L05 - Scientific Realism and its Critiques|L05]] score.

**For realism, each with "some force":** inference to the best explanation (van Fraassen: there is no such rule, and "the theory is empirically adequate", i.e. right about the observable phenomena, is an equally good rival hypothesis); the no-miracles argument (success, above all novel prediction, would be a miracle if theories were not approximately true); the demand for explanation (Musgrave against van Fraassen, mock Q8 in L05); and the lack of a sharp line between theory and observation.

**Against realism:**

- **Empirical under-determination** (the same evidence fits rival theories): a **stalemate, not much force**. Logically different theories with the same predictions are easy to build, but they are toy models; real cases in physics are rare and controversial. Realists say **theoretical virtues** (simplicity, beauty, fruitfulness) break the tie, being non-empirical but **truth-conducive**; the anti-realist denies that simpler means more likely true, and neither side can force the other. (More in [[PhilSci-L03 - Under-determination|L03]].)
- **Constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen: science aims only at empirical adequacy): the **main contender**. It avoids the problems of logical empiricism and keeps some virtues of realism, but where "observable" ends **remains a problem**.
- **The pessimistic meta-induction**: **some force**. The rest of this note is about it.

## The challenge, restated

Reminders from L05:

- **The pessimistic meta-induction (PMI, Laudan 1981):** many once-successful theories posited things we now think do not exist, so by an induction over theories (hence "meta"), our successful theories will probably turn out false too.
- **Laudan's list:** crystalline spheres, humoral medicine, phlogiston, caloric, the optical and electromagnetic aether and more, "in every case a theory which was once successful and well confirmed, but which contained central terms which (we now believe) were non-referring".
- **Convergent realism**, his target: successive mature theories are approximately true, keep the reference of their terms, and converge on the truth.
- **Saatsi's two readings:** the PMI predicts that current theories are false, or, more modestly, shows that success is not a reliable sign of truth.

As problems for the realist:

1. There is no good notion of **approximate truth**.
2. There is no connection between **empirical success** and approximate truth.
3. **Truth requires reference** (a claim can only be true of what its words pick out), but old theories contain **obviously non-referring** terms.

Keep an eye on "obviously": De Haro denies it.

### What realists actually claim

Kitcher (1993) and Psillos (1999) correct the target first. Van Fraassen defined realism as: "Science aims to give us, in its theories, a **literally true** story of what the world is like; and acceptance of a scientific theory involves the belief that it is true." No serious realist believes that: realists believe theories are **approximately or probably true**.

And a theory is **not monolithic**. It has parts (laws, mechanisms, posited entities), some more important than others. That suggests an answer to Laudan's first problem: "approximately true" means the important parts are true even if others are false, which can be checked part by part.

## First reply: only mature theories count

Boyd, and separately Hardin and Rosenberg (1981): be a realist only about **mature**, well-confirmed theories. Psillos spells out "mature": the discipline has passed a **take-off point**, where well-entrenched background beliefs fix the domain and constrain new theories (for heat: no perpetual motion, heat flows from warm to cold, Newtonian mechanics). Humoral medicine and effluvial electricity then drop off Laudan's list.

**Stanford (2006):** the maturity requirement is **arbitrary and post hoc**, and some listed theories surely were well confirmed: the **phlogiston theory of combustion** and the **caloric theory of heat**.

Psillos concedes this himself: caloric theory and the nineteenth-century optical aether theories were mature and successful, and are still considered false. Maturity shrinks the list without defeating the PMI.

## Second reply: believe only the parts that did the work

**Selective confirmation:** success confirms some parts of a theory and not others, so realism should be selective too.

### Kitcher: reference is fixed case by case

Philip Kitcher argues that scientists' terms **do not automatically refer**; look at the context of each use. "Aether" might refer to the **electromagnetic field** on some occasions, to **empty space** on others, and to **nothing at all** on others. So Laudan is wrong to conclude that such terms simply fail to refer.

Reference attaches to each **token** (one occasion of using a word), guided by the speaker's intention then. Some of Priestley's uses of "dephlogisticated air" pick out what we call oxygen, and those refer.

**Stanford's reply:** perhaps, but it misses the point of the meta-induction. Even if the central terms refer, they sit inside **theories that repeatedly turn out radically misguided**, and such theories cannot be approximately true. It is "little comfort to the realist" if those tokens referred, "while admitting that the relevant theoretical accounts and descriptions of those entities were mistaken about virtually everything except the fact that the entities in question played some causal role in producing observable phenomena".

Realism needs approximate truth, and reference is only a precondition for it. Saving reference while the descriptions were almost all wrong is a win on a technicality.

### Psillos: divide et impera

*Divide et impera* is Latin for "divide and rule".

> [!definition] Psillos' selective confirmation
> - **Causal core:** the parts that explain the phenomena and are **responsible for the theory's success**. Typically **retained** by later theories.
> - **Idle constituents:** parts **not causally involved** in explaining the phenomena. Typically **disappear** in later theories.

Success confirms only the core, so if the abandoned posits were idle, their loss says nothing about the parts that earned the success. To find the core, take a specific success and ask which assumptions "really fuel the derivation": those the prediction cannot be derived without, and that no independently motivated, non-ad-hoc alternative could replace.

> [!example] Maxwell's theory and the aether
> The causal core of Maxwell's electromagnetism is its **equations and mechanisms of explanation**, which do not require the aether. The aether is an **idle constituent**, not retained in modern theory. So Maxwell's success confirms the equations, and dropping the aether shows nothing against them.

**Compare Lakatos** ([[PhilSci-L01b - Popper and Lakatos|L01b]]): a research programme has a protected **hard core** and a **protective belt** that gets adjusted and abandoned. Both split a theory into a part that carries the weight and a part that can go, on different grounds: Lakatos' hard core is what scientists decide to protect; Psillos' causal core is defined by its role in producing success.

### Which theory of reference?

Whether "aether" refers depends on what fixes reference.

- **Descriptive theories:** a term refers to whatever fits the theory's description. When descriptions change, reference changes, so every case on Laudan's list fails to refer. The PMI quietly assumes this.
- **Causal theories** (Kripke, Putnam): a term refers to whatever caused the phenomena that led scientists to introduce it. "Aether" then refers to the field, which carries electromagnetic energy. Complaint: this makes reference **too easy**.
- **Mixed theories** (Papineau, Lewis, Psillos): a term refers to an entity that **both causes the phenomena and satisfies a causal description** of how it does so. The causal contact anchors the description; the description stops reference coming too cheaply.

## Third reply: keep only the mathematics

**Structural realism:** what survives theory change is the **mathematical structure**. The equations carry over even when the entities they were about are dropped. (A standard example, from outside the readings: Fresnel's equations for light survived into Maxwell's theory, Fresnel's elastic aether did not.)

**Objection:** structure may **not be enough to specify an ontology**. You can say the equations are approximately right, but not what kinds of things the world contains, which was the point of realism.

## Stanford: a Pyrrhic victory

Kyle Stanford's **historicist** critique (one that tests philosophy of science against the actual history) makes four points against selective confirmation.

1. **The core/idle split is anachronistic.** The scientists would have denied it: **Maxwell regarded the aether as essential** to electromagnetic theory. How can we say his theory "was really not about the aether"?
2. **Convergence is virtually guaranteed.** Almost by definition, whatever current theories retain is what we call the "causal core", so the core's survival is built in, and it need not be what past scientists thought mattered.
3. **We need prospectively applicable criteria.** A core found by looking back at what survived works only on past theories. For our own we would need to know which parts will survive, which is what we do not know; future scientists will sort ours with hindsight as we sort Maxwell's. Otherwise it is **Whig history** (the past written as a march towards the present, judged by what we now believe), and we are not entitled to realism about our best theories.
4. **The dilemma.** Selective confirmation requires **either** facts about past scientists' beliefs and intentions that we do not have, **or** saying they repeatedly misidentified the parts of their theories that produced success.

> [!definition] Pyrrhic victory
> A victory so costly it is no better than defeat. Selective realism saves the approximate truth of past theories only by overruling the scientists who built them, or by claiming access to their minds. Realism set out to trust what successful science says; overruling the scientists gives that up.

**Psillos' answer, given in advance:** scientists draw the core/idle line themselves, at the time. Lavoisier, Laplace and Carnot believed the caloric laws that fuelled their predictions while treating "heat is a material fluid" as speculative. Stanford's aether case is aimed at exactly this: Maxwell did not treat the aether as idle.

Stanford's book is named after a second argument, the **problem of unconceived alternatives** (background, not developed in the readings): past scientists repeatedly failed to think of equally good rival theories that were adopted later, so we probably have not thought of ours. De Haro's view is offered as a reply to both.

## De Haro's extensional scientific realism

### What De Haro takes from Stanford, and what he rejects

Stanford's critique **seems generally on the mark**. Two lessons stand:

1. A solution must **explain the connection between reference and approximate truth**, giving an account of both.
2. It must **not project current insights into the past**, such as what we now think a theory "is really about". Read past scientists' arguments **in their historical context**.

Two charges are rejected:

- **Convergence is not guaranteed.** Strip a theory of its idle terms and it could happen that **none of the original terms remain**. So a core that survives is **highly remarkable**, a real finding about history.
- **Stanford reads into Maxwell's text** when he concludes that Maxwell thought energy transmission without an aether "incoherent" and "unintelligible".

So Stanford's critique of continuity **is not strong**, but his two demands stand. De Haro's realism is cautious: belief in literal truth is often naive, the claim is approximate truth, and the first aim is a cogent answer to the PMI.

### The diagnosis: one word, two kinds of meaning

The literature from Putnam (1978, who first stated the PMI) to Stanford assumes **a single kind of meaning** of words. Hence Laudan's perplexity about old terms "failing to refer": there are plausible arguments both that "aether" refers (Kitcher, causal theories) and that it does not (descriptive theories). When both sides look right, a distinction is missing. Philosophy of language (Frege, Carnap, Lewis) already has it: **two types of meaning**.

> [!example] The morning star and the evening star
> Venus is seen both in the morning and in the evening, and was called both "morning star" and "evening star". The names **mean different things**, "the brightest star in the morning" and "the brightest star in the evening", yet in our world both pick out **one object, the planet Venus**. That they coincide had to be discovered.

> [!definition] Sense and reference (intension and extension)
> - **Sense = intension:** the **linguistic meaning** of the words. *Morning star; evening star.*
> - **Reference = extension:** the **actual object or entity** the words refer to. *Venus.*
>
> "Sense" and "reference" are Frege's terms; "intension" and "extension" are **Carnap's** words for the same distinction.

Two terms are **extensionally equivalent** when they pick out the same thing in our world, even if their intensions differ.

### Temperature: different intensions, one extension

- **A lay person:** temperature is "the expansion of mercury that I measure with a thermometer".
- **A thermodynamicist** (the science of heat and energy flow): "a measure of the tendency of an object to give energy to its surroundings". Hot things give heat off; cold things take it in.
- **A statistical physicist** (heat as molecular motion): "the mean kinetic energy of the molecules in a substance". Hot means fast molecules.

The two physicists give "temperature" **different intensions**, because their definitions differ, but both pick out **the same physical quantity**: one extension.

This defuses Kuhn's claim that scientists in different paradigms "live in different worlds" ([[PhilSci-L02 - Kuhn on Scientific Practice|L02]]). With two kinds of meaning, the change can be located: the **intension** changed, the **extension** did not. The two physicists disagree about what temperature *is*, and still measure and predict the same quantity.

### The answer: believe extensions, within the domain

The PMI denies **continuity of reference and approximate truth** between discarded theories and their successors. De Haro answers in two steps.

1. **Continuity of extensions.** Successive theories are **extensionally equivalent**: they refer to the same items in their **domain of application** (the situations where the theory has been well tested). Example: all the extensional results of classical mechanics can be derived from quantum mechanics. Unlike Psillos, continuity comes not from **theory parts** but from **delimiting a theory's extension to its domain of application**.
2. **Approximate truth in terms of extensions** (last subsection).

> [!definition] The slogan of extensional scientific realism
> *We are justified in being scientific realists about extensions but not necessarily about intensions.*

**Why stop at extensions?** An intension covers every situation the theory could be applied to, including many where it was never tested and may be false. The evidence supports only the extension within the domain. Prudent scientists never claim more: general relativity is expected to fail at very small length scales, and effective quantum field theories come with a cut-off beyond which they need replacing. So a successor that says something radically different **outside** the old domain is no threat: nobody had reason to believe the old theory there. Continuity is needed only where the domains overlap. De Haro's example: for planetary motions, Newton's "gravitational force" and general relativity's spacetime curvature cause the same phenomena (the Newtonian force can be derived from general relativity), so there they are extensionally equivalent.

**What fixes an extension?** "Morning star" and "evening star" coincide in our world, under our conditions of observation.

> [!definition] What determines an extension
> The extension is determined by the **intension** together with the **specific circumstances or context** in which a phenomenon is studied.

In natural science the circumstances are: the **model** specified (initial and boundary conditions, environment); the **values of free parameters** (mass, coupling strength, concentration); **extra-theoretical facts** (experimental error, other influences, weather); and **approximations and idealisations**, such as the limits $v \ll c$ (speeds far below light speed $c$, where relativity reduces to Newton), $\hbar \to 0$ (Planck's constant to zero, quantum effects vanish) and $G \to 0$ (gravitational constant to zero, gravity off).

So the domain is no fudge letting the realist retreat wherever a theory fails: it is fixed by models, parameters, error margins and limits that scientists already state. (Compare **effective realism** in high-energy physics: realism about a theory in a specified range of parameters.) Intensions still matter, since they help fix the extensions: imperfect intensions, corrected by the right circumstances, deliver correct extensions.

### How to show two theories share an extension

> [!definition] Three kinds of correspondence
> 1. **Predictive (numerical and formal) correspondence:** the two theories make the **same predictions under the relevant approximations**: the same values (numerical), and laws of the same form (formal).
> 2. **Material correspondence** (including experimental and instrumental correspondence, and replicability): **the system studied is the same**, seen from different points of view.
> 3. **Conceptual correspondence:** the concepts are **not identical but "match"**: they play the same roles in the given extension.

- **Predictive:** quantum mechanics reproduces classical mechanics under specific conditions (large systems, among others):
$$\left\langle \frac{dp}{dt} \right\rangle = -\langle \nabla V \rangle \quad\longrightarrow\quad F = -\nabla V$$
where $p$ is momentum, $V$ potential energy, $\nabla V$ its gradient, $\langle \cdot \rangle$ the quantum average over the state, and $F$ the classical force. The quantum law for averages has the form of Newton's law.
- **Material:** the **atom** studied by the quantum theorist and by the inorganic chemist is the same entity, though their experiments differ (particle accelerator, electron microscope, chemical reaction).
- **Conceptual:** both theories have a notion of **"position of a particle"**, and on a given extension the two play the same role.

Material correspondence alone is not enough: two descriptions of the same sample that ascribed conflicting measurable properties would not count as equivalent. Predictive correspondence is needed too.

### The aether exists

The pessimist's strongest case: **"aether" does not refer**, so how can the old electromagnetic theory be true, or even approximately true? Stanford calls its ontology "radically misguided". Old and new theories are in predictive and material correspondence, but apparently **not conceptual**.

De Haro: **"aether" does refer. The aether does exist.**

> [!definition] What "aether" refers to
> What Maxwell called the "aether" is **extensionally equivalent** to what we now call **"the electromagnetic field", together with a fixed frame of reference**, $v \ll c$. "Aether" and "electromagnetic field" are, of course, **intensionally distinct**.

**Maxwell's argument for the aether was very good.** Electromagnetic energy does not vanish at the sender and pop up at the receiver elsewhere (**conservation of energy**), so something in between carries it. The "medium" he argued for is the electromagnetic field. Read this way, the conceptual correspondence is there after all: in the domain, Maxwell's medium and our field play the same role.

Why the fixed frame and $v \ll c$ is argued in De Haro's companion paper, which is not among the readings, so it is not settled here. A plausible reading (background physics): a medium has a state of rest, so it singles out one frame of reference, while the modern field singles out none; in one fixed frame at speeds far below light, the two descriptions predict the same.

This also answers Stanford's anachronism charge. Psillos had to call the aether idle, against Maxwell. De Haro agrees with Maxwell that the aether was essential, real and well argued for; what the argument established is, extensionally, the field.

### No parts, so no Pyrrhic victory

Stanford's dilemma came from sorting theories into good and bad parts. **Extensional realism sorts no parts.** It is a linguistic and philosophical theory of **how to interpret scientific statements**, treating all terms alike: assuming empirically adequate theories and sound arguments, **all the terms that scientists claim refer, do refer**. It needs no facts about hidden intentions and never says scientists misidentified their successes. The only hindsight it needs is benign: finding the exact borders of a domain, often only when a theory fails there. Newton already secured reference for "gravitational force" in the domain where he had applied his theory.

**The parallel with van Fraassen:** he interprets a theory **literally** but believes only what it says about the **observable**. Likewise, extensional realism takes literally what the scientist says, but **believes it only in its extensional, not intensional, meaning**.

> [!warning] Extensional realism is still realism
> The shape is shared (literal reading, restricted belief); the restriction differs. Van Fraassen's line falls between observable and unobservable, so electrons are out. De Haro's falls between extension and intension, and electrons, forces and fields are extensions, so they are in.

### Approximate truth as a larger domain

The last step answers Laudan's first problem, using **Laudan's own measure of scientific progress**: the relative number and significance of the problems a theory solves.

> [!definition] Closer to the truth (De Haro's sketch)
> $T$ is closer to the truth than $T'$ **iff** $T$ is **extensionally true in a larger, and more significant, domain of application**.

**Worry:** could a theory cover more by saying less, growing vaguer as it grows more general? The standard answer: $T$ must also be **as good a theory as $T'$**, for example in predictive power and explanation.

> [!example] Comparing two theories
> An old theory is extensionally true on domains $D_1$ and $D_2$. Its successor is true on larger domains $D_1' \supset D_1$ and $D_2' \supset D_2$, and on a further domain $D_3$. A domain $D_4$ is covered by neither. The successor is closer to the truth, and the old theory is **not refuted**: it was, and still is, extensionally true on $D_1$ and $D_2$, which is the sense in which it is approximately true.

**The upshot.** The perplexity about reference comes from too simple readings of the history of science that ignore intension and extension. Stanford is partly right, but his "broad pattern(s) of repeated, profound, and unpredictable changes in fundamental theoretical orthodoxy" misses the **restricted domains of application** where successive theories are extensionally equivalent: changes in intension can be radical, changes in extension are piecemeal. And Laudan's three problems each get an answer: approximate truth has a definition; success connects to reference through extensions in the domain where it was won; and the aether, his flagship example, refers.

## Are we in the same position as past scientists?

The PMI assumes our situation is like that of past scientists. The positions differ on that:

- **Laudan**, with Mary Hesse's "principle of no privilege": yes, our theories are as liable to radical change as past ones.
- **Psillos:** not quite. Scientists learn from failures and improve their methods, and many posits survived several revolutions: the gene, the atom, the chemical bond, the electromagnetic field.
- **Stanford:** yes. Future scientists will judge ours with hindsight, as we judge Maxwell's, and we have probably not conceived of the alternatives.
- **De Haro:** past scientists were justified only within their domains, and there their beliefs mostly survived. What changed were claims about untested domains, which nobody was justified in believing.

> - So it never overrules the scientists: it can agree with Maxwell that the aether was essential and real. The only hindsight it needs is benign: where a domain's borders lie.
> - **Parallel:** van Fraassen reads theories literally but believes only what they say about the observable; De Haro takes literally what the scientist says but believes it only in its **extensional**, not intensional, meaning.
> - **Difference:** extensions include unobservables (fields, electrons, forces), so extensional realism is a realism; the observable/unobservable line plays no role.

> [!exam]- Practice question: Is Laudan right that our current epistemic predicament is analogous to that of past scientists? Discuss with reference to the replies to the pessimistic meta-induction.
> **Key points:** The PMI needs the analogy (principle of no privilege); Psillos: better methods and many surviving posits; Stanford: future scientists will judge us with hindsight, unconceived alternatives; De Haro: justified belief was always restricted to domains, and there it survived; a judgement of your own.
>
> - **Why it matters:** the PMI infers our fate from past scientists' fate, so it needs our situation to be relevantly like theirs (Hesse's "principle of no privilege").
> - **Against the analogy (Psillos):** scientists learn from failures and improve methods and evidence, so newer beliefs are better supported; and many posits have survived several revolutions (gene, atom, chemical bond, electromagnetic field), so Laudan overstates the discontinuity.
> - **For the analogy (Stanford):** realists can only pick out what survives with hindsight, and future scientists will do the same to our theories; we have probably not conceived of the alternatives that will replace them.
> - **Reframing (De Haro):** the analogy is harmless once belief is restricted to extensions in a domain of application. Past scientists' justified beliefs, within their domains, mostly survived (Newtonian gravity, Maxwell's field); what changed were intensions in untested domains, which nobody was justified in believing.
> - A good answer takes a side, for example: the analogy holds for intensions and fails for extensions.

## Flashcards

> [!card]- How much force do the four main arguments for scientific realism (IBE, no-miracles, demand for explanation, no theory-observation line) have, on balance?
> **Some force** each, none decisive.

> [!card]- How strong is empirical under-determination as an argument against scientific realism?
> A **stalemate**, with not much force.

> [!card]- Why is the empirical under-determination argument against realism a stalemate?
> Anti-realists lack real, non-toy cases of equivalent rivals; realists cannot show theoretical virtues are **truth-conducive**.

> [!card]- How does the realist claim to break empirical under-determination?
> With **theoretical virtues** (simplicity, beauty, fruitfulness): non-empirical but truth-conducive, so equivalent theories still differ.

> [!card]- Which anti-realist position is the main contender against scientific realism?
> **Constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen), though "observable" remains a problem for it.

> [!card]- What are the three problems the pessimistic meta-induction raises for realism?
> No good notion of **approximate truth**; no link from success to approximate truth; truth needs **reference**, yet old terms seem obviously non-referring.

> [!card]- What do Kitcher and Psillos say realists believe about successful theories, against van Fraassen's definition of realism?
> Their **approximate or probable truth**, not their literal truth.

> [!card]- How does treating theories as having "parts" help with Laudan's approximate-truth problem?
> "Approximately true" can mean the **important parts are true** even if others are false, checkable part by part.

> [!card]- What is the "mature theories only" reply to the pessimistic meta-induction (Boyd, Hardin and Rosenberg)?
> Be a realist only about **mature, well-confirmed** theories; the immature ones on Laudan's list do not count.

> [!card]- What makes a discipline mature, according to Psillos?
> It has passed a **take-off point**: well-entrenched background beliefs fix the domain and constrain new theories.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's objection to the "mature theories only" reply?
> Maturity is **arbitrary** and post hoc, and some listed theories were well confirmed: **phlogiston** and **caloric**.

> [!card]- Why does Psillos himself think restricting realism to mature theories does not defeat the pessimistic meta-induction?
> **Caloric** theory and nineteenth-century **optical aether** theories were mature and successful yet are considered false.

> [!card]- How does Kitcher reply to Laudan's claim that terms like "aether" were non-referring?
> Terms do not automatically refer: **reference is fixed differently in different contexts**, so Laudan's conclusion is wrong.

> [!card]- On Kitcher's account, what three things could "aether" refer to in different cases?
> The **electromagnetic field**, **empty space**, or **nothing at all**.

> [!card]- What fixes the reference of a token of a term, on Kitcher's account?
> The **speaker's intention** on that occasion of use.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's objection to Kitcher's account of the reference of old theoretical terms?
> Even if the terms refer, the theories were **radically misguided**, so cannot be approximately true.

> [!card]- Why does saving reference not save scientific realism, according to Stanford's reply to Kitcher?
> Realism needs **approximate truth**, and reference is only a **precondition** for it.

> [!card]- In Psillos' divide et impera, what is the causal core of a theory?
> The part that **explains the phenomena** and is **responsible for the theory's success**; typically retained later.

> [!card]- What does Psillos call the parts of a theory that play no role in its success and typically disappear later?
> **Idle constituents**.

> [!card]- When does an assumption "really fuel" a successful prediction, for Psillos?
> When the rest of the theory cannot yield the prediction without it, and **no non-ad-hoc alternative** could replace it.

> [!card]- How does Psillos apply divide et impera to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory?
> The equations and mechanisms of explanation are the **causal core** and need no aether; the **aether is idle**.

> [!card]- What is the difference between Psillos' causal core and Lakatos' hard core?
> Lakatos' hard core is what scientists **decide to protect**; Psillos' causal core is defined by its **role in producing success**.

> [!card]- Why does a purely descriptive theory of reference favour Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction?
> A term refers to whatever fits the description, so when **descriptions change, reference changes**.

> [!card]- What is the complaint against purely causal theories of reference?
> They make reference **too easy**: almost any abandoned term, like "aether", ends up referring.

> [!card]- On Psillos' mixed theory of reference, when does a term refer to an entity?
> When the entity both **causes the phenomena** and **satisfies a causal description** of how it causes them.

> [!card]- According to structural realism, what is preserved across theory change?
> **Mathematical structure** (the equations), even when the posited entities are dropped.

> [!card]- What is the main objection to structural realism as a reply to the pessimistic meta-induction?
> Preserved structure may not be enough to specify an **ontology**: what kinds of things the world contains.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's anachronism objection to Psillos' selective confirmation?
> The core/idle split would have been **denied by the scientists themselves**: Maxwell regarded the aether as **essential**.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford say convergence on the causal core is "virtually guaranteed" in selective realism?
> **Almost by definition**, whatever current theories retain counts as the causal core.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford demand "prospectively applicable" criteria of idleness?
> Hindsight sorting works only on past theories; it cannot tell which parts of **our current theories** to believe.

> [!card]- What is Whig history?
> Writing the past as a march towards the present, **judged by what we now believe**.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's dilemma for selective confirmation?
> Either know the **beliefs and intentions of past scientists**, or say they **repeatedly misidentified** what produced their success.

> [!card]- Why does Stanford call selective realism's win over the pessimistic meta-induction a "Pyrrhic victory"?
> Either horn costs too much: it must **overrule the scientists** realism set out to trust, or claim access to their **beliefs** we lack.

> [!card]- How does Psillos answer the objection that the core/idle line is drawn only with hindsight?
> Scientists drew it **at the time**: Lavoisier, Laplace and Carnot believed the caloric laws but doubted heat was a fluid.

> [!card]- What is Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives?
> Past scientists repeatedly missed equally good alternative theories adopted later, so we probably have **unconceived alternatives** too.

> [!card]- Which two lessons does De Haro accept from Stanford's critique of selective confirmation?
> Explain **reference and approximate truth** together, and **do not project current insights into the past**.

> [!card]- Why does De Haro deny that convergence on a causal core is "virtually guaranteed"?
> Eliminating idle terms could leave **none of the original terms**, so a surviving core is highly remarkable.

> [!card]- What is De Haro's second criticism of Stanford?
> Stanford **reads into Maxwell's text** that energy transmission without an aether was "incoherent" and "unintelligible".

> [!card]- What weakness does De Haro find in the realism literature from Putnam to Stanford?
> It assumes **a single kind of meaning** of words, which produces Laudan's perplexity about reference.

> [!card]- Which premise of the pessimistic meta-induction does De Haro attack directly?
> That old theories contain **obviously non-referring** terms. De Haro: not at all obvious.

> [!card]- What is the intension (Frege's sense) of a word?
> Its **linguistic meaning**.

> [!card]- What is the extension (Frege's reference) of a word?
> The **actual object or entity** the word refers to.

> [!card]- What does the "morning star" and "evening star" example illustrate?
> **Two different senses, one reference**: both names pick out Venus.

> [!card]- How do the thermodynamic and statistical-mechanical definitions of temperature differ?
> Thermodynamics: tendency of an object to **give energy to its surroundings**. Statistical mechanics: **mean kinetic energy of molecules**.

> [!card]- On De Haro's account, what do the thermodynamic and statistical-mechanical uses of "temperature" share?
> The same **extension** (one physical quantity), despite different **intensions**.

> [!card]- How does the intension/extension distinction reinterpret Kuhn's claim that scientists in different paradigms "live in different worlds"?
> The **intension** changes between theories, the **extension** need not: both still refer to the same thing.

> [!card]- What are the two steps of De Haro's answer to the pessimistic meta-induction?
> 1. Continuity of **extensions** (extensional equivalence). 2. Define **approximate truth** in terms of extensions.

> [!card]- What is extensional equivalence between two theories, for De Haro?
> Reference to the **same items in the domain of application**.

> [!card]- How does De Haro's extensional equivalence secure continuity differently from Psillos' selective confirmation?
> Psillos: via **theory parts** (retained core). De Haro: by **delimiting the extension to a domain of application**.

> [!card]- What is the slogan of De Haro's extensional scientific realism?
> We are justified in being realists about **extensions** but not necessarily about **intensions**.

> [!card]- Why should a realist commit only to a theory's extension in its domain of application, according to De Haro?
> The intension covers **untested situations** where the theory may be false; evidence supports only the tested extension.

> [!card]- On De Haro's account, why is a successor theory's radical difference outside the old theory's domain no threat to realism?
> Nobody had **good reason to believe** the old theory outside its domain; continuity is needed only on the overlap.

> [!card]- Which Newtonian concept does De Haro call extensionally equivalent to spacetime curvature in its domain?
> The **gravitational force**: both cause the same phenomena for nearby gravitation.

> [!card]- What determines the extension of a term in a scientific theory, according to De Haro?
> The **intension** plus the **specific circumstances or context** in which the phenomenon is studied.

> [!card]- What four kinds of circumstance determine extensions in natural science, for De Haro?
> Model specification, **free parameter values**, extra-theoretical facts, **approximations and idealisations**.

> [!card]- What role do intensions keep in De Haro's extensional realism?
> They help **determine the extensions**: imperfect intensions, corrected by circumstances, deliver correct extensions.

> [!card]- What three kinds of correspondence establish extensional equivalence, for De Haro?
> **Predictive** (numerical and formal), **material**, **conceptual**.

> [!card]- What is conceptual correspondence between two theories, for De Haro?
> The concepts are **not identical** but **match**: they play the same roles in the given extension.

> [!card]- Quantum expectation values obey the form of Newton's $F = -\nabla V$. Which kind of De Haro correspondence is this?
> **Predictive** (numerical and formal) correspondence.

> [!card]- The quantum theorist and the inorganic chemist study the same atom with different experiments. Which kind of De Haro correspondence is this?
> **Material** correspondence.

> [!card]- "Position of a particle" plays the same role in quantum and classical mechanics. Which kind of De Haro correspondence is this?
> **Conceptual** correspondence.

> [!card]- Why is material correspondence alone not enough for extensional equivalence?
> Two descriptions of the same sample could ascribe **conflicting measurable properties**; predictive correspondence is needed too.

> [!card]- On the pessimist's account of aether theory, which kind of correspondence with modern electromagnetism is missing?
> **Conceptual**; predictive and material correspondence hold.

> [!card]- According to De Haro, what is Maxwell's "aether" extensionally equivalent to?
> The **electromagnetic field together with a fixed frame of reference**, $v \ll c$.

> [!card]- What was Maxwell's argument for the existence of the aether, which De Haro calls very good?
> **Conservation of energy**: energy cannot vanish at the sender and reappear at the receiver, so something carries it.

> [!card]- According to De Haro, are "aether" and "electromagnetic field" the same in intension?
> **No**: intensionally distinct, extensionally equivalent in the domain.

> [!card]- How does De Haro's extensional realism escape Stanford's "Pyrrhic victory" dilemma?
> It **distinguishes no theory parts**: assuming sound arguments, all terms scientists claim refer do refer.

> [!card]- How does De Haro's extensional realism answer Stanford's anachronism objection about Maxwell?
> It **agrees with Maxwell** that the aether was essential and real; his argument established, extensionally, the field.

> [!card]- What kind of hindsight does De Haro admit extensional realism needs?
> Only **benign** hindsight: finding the exact borders of a theory's domain of application.

> [!card]- What do De Haro's extensional realism and van Fraassen's constructive empiricism have in common?
> Both **read theories literally** but **restrict belief**: van Fraassen to the observable, De Haro to extensions.

> [!card]- Why is De Haro's extensional realism still a realism, unlike constructive empiricism?
> Extensions include **unobservables** (fields, electrons); the observable/unobservable line plays no role.

> [!card]- What is the difference between Psillos' selective realism and De Haro's extensional realism?
> Psillos believes **some parts** of a theory; De Haro believes the **whole theory, but only extensionally** in its domain.

> [!card]- What is the difference between how Kitcher and De Haro save the reference of old terms like "aether"?
> Kitcher saves it **token by token**; De Haro saves it **for all terms alike**, within the domain.

> [!card]- In De Haro's sketch, when is theory $T$ closer to the truth than $T'$?
> Iff $T$ is **extensionally true in a larger, and more significant, domain of application**.

> [!card]- Whose idea of scientific progress does De Haro use to define approximate truth?
> **Laudan's**: progress measured by the relative number and significance of the problems a theory solves.

> [!card]- In De Haro's account, what stops a theory counting as closer to the truth merely by becoming more general and vaguer?
> It must also be **as good a theory** as its rival, e.g. in **predictive power and explanation**.

> [!card]- On De Haro's account, is an old theory refuted when a successor is extensionally true on a larger domain?
> **No**: it remains extensionally true, so approximately true, on its own domain.

> [!card]- What does De Haro say Stanford's "repeated, profound, and unpredictable changes" in theory miss?
> **Restricted domains of application** where successive theories are **extensionally equivalent**.

> [!card]- What is Mary Hesse's "principle of no privilege"?
> Our theories are **as liable to radical conceptual change** as past theories were.

> [!card]- Why does Psillos deny that our position is just like that of past scientists?
> Scientists **learn and improve methods**, and many posits (gene, atom, electromagnetic field) **survived** several revolutions.
