> [!exam]- Mock exam Q8: "One of the arguments discussed by van Fraassen and Musgrave, in their debate on scientific realism, is about scientific explanation. Van Fraassen attacks the realist demand for explanation, and argues that explanation is not one of the aims of science. Musgrave then responds that van Fraassen tacitly conflates 'realism with essentialism, ... the demand for explanation with the demand for ultimate explanation'. What does Musgrave mean by this, and how is it a reply to van Fraassen?" (10 points)
> Follow the official key (Musgrave, pp. 1102-1103):
> 1. **Ultimate** scientific explanations serve to **remove puzzlement**.
> 2. **Non-ultimate** scientific explanations do not serve this pragmatic function: they **relocate and enhance** puzzlement. Yet they are **real explanations**.
> 3. There can always be **better or deeper** explanations, and scientific theories certainly **do aim to give explanations**, but there is **no unlimited demand** for explanations.
>
> How it answers van Fraassen (a reconstruction from the question and the key; Musgrave's paper has not been checked, so this part is not settled): van Fraassen argues the realist's demand for explanation cannot be unlimited and concludes explanation is no aim of science. That only follows if "explanation" means **ultimate** explanation, the **essentialist** ideal of explanation bottoming out in essences. Since non-ultimate explanations are genuine, science can aim at better and deeper explanations without an unlimited demand for ultimate ones. That removes van Fraassen's reason for denying explanation is an aim of science, and with it his reason for dismissing IBE.

> [!exam]- What does scientific realism claim, and what are the two main arguments for it? Present them and say where an anti-realist pushes back.
> - **Claim:** empirically well-confirmed theories are true or approximately true, **including what they say about unobservables**. This goes beyond empirical adequacy.
> - It is the **conjunction** of three theses: **semantic** (read theories literally), **epistemic** (we are justified in accepting them as true), **metaphysical** (the world is as the theory says; its entities exist). Rejecting any one makes you an anti-realist.
> - **No-miracles argument (Putnam):** "Realism is the only philosophy that does not make the success of science a miracle." Novel predictions such as the **Higgs boson** and the **top quark**, made on theoretical grounds before any experiment, would be incredible coincidences if the theories were false.
> - **IBE:** the NMA is an inference to the best explanation. Explanandum: theories successfully predict experiments never performed. Best explanation: their approximate truth. Conclusion: sufficiently successful theories are (probably) approximately true.
> - **Pushback:** anti-realists often deny explanation is an aim of science, so "best explanation" gives no reason to believe. Van Fraassen offers a rival hypothesis (we believe the best explanation is **empirically adequate**). Laudan's PMI shows success has gone with falsity before.

> [!exam]- State van Fraassen's constructive empiricism and explain how it survives Maxwell's objection that the theory-observation distinction cannot be drawn sharply.
> - **Constructive empiricism:** "Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate." Contrast realism: the aim is "a literally true story", and acceptance involves belief that the theory is true.
> - **Position:** semantic realist (claims about unobservables are literally true or false), epistemic and metaphysical anti-realist. Belief about unobservables is "up to us". He is agnostic about electrons; he does not say they do not exist.
> - **Maxwell's threat:** glasses, window, microscope lie on a continuum with no principled cut-off. Fatal to the positivist, who tied meaning (all-or-nothing) to the line.
> - **Three moves:** (1) take theories **literally** in all respects, dropping verifiability; (2) the distinction is a **category mistake**: theoretical/non-theoretical **terms** (language) versus observable/unobservable **entities** (objects); (3) "observable" is **vague but usable**, like "bald".
> - **Why it works:** only epistemic commitment hangs on the line, so a vague boundary is harmless. Clear cases on each side (a table, an electron) are all the argument needs.
>
> Losing marks: calling van Fraassen a positivist, or saying he denies unobservables exist.

> [!exam]- Present Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction against convergent realism, and explain Saatsi's two readings of it.
> - **Target, convergent realism:** mature theories are approximately true, refer and preserve reference across theory change, and new theories explain the success of old ones, so science converges on the truth.
> - **PMI:** many once empirically successful theories are now considered false; they took terms like **"aether"** and **"phlogiston"** to refer, and we now take those terms not to refer. By induction over theories, present successful theories may well turn out false too, so we are not justified in believing theories true or their posits real.
> - **Three problems (Laudan 1981):** (1) no good definition of **approximate truth**; (2) no justification for the realist's link **success → approximate truth → reference**; (3) central terms of once-successful theories do not refer, so those theories were not approximately true, yet were successful. All three stem from approximate truth.
> - **Saatsi (2005):** reading 1, an induction on the past (current theories are likely false); reading 2, a timeless argument that undermines the NMA by showing **success is not a reliable indicator of truth**. Reading 2 is more modest and harder to resist: even if our situation differs from the 1800s, past successful-but-false theories make the NMA's "miracles" less miraculous.
>
> Losing marks: treating the PMI as an induction over observations, or as just "science has been wrong before" without the target link success → approximate truth → reference.

> [!card]- What is the ontological question of realism, and how do metaphysical realism and Berkeley's idealism answer it?
> The question: does the external world **exist independently of us**, of our thoughts and perceptions?
> - **Metaphysical realism** (most philosophers from Plato to the present): yes, things exist independently of the mind.
> - **Idealism** (Berkeley): no, the world is mind-dependent. *Esse est percipi*, "to be is to be perceived": there is nothing more to a thing existing than its being perceived, and what is not perceived does not exist.

> [!card]- Why is Berkeley's idealism called "the maximally empiricist possible answer"?
> Our **only** access to anything beyond ourselves is through perception, so we have no way of checking whether anything exists beyond perception. If you limit yourself strictly to what you can perceive and check, there is nothing beyond your perceptions. Considerations like this push people towards a middle ground between realism and idealism.

> [!card]- What is Kant's compromise between realism and idealism? Name the question he moves to, the two realms, and the mind's structures.
> Kant moves from the ontological to the **epistemological question**: what knowledge of the external world can we have?
> - There **is** an external reality, the **noumena** (things-in-themselves), but we cannot know it as it is in itself.
> - We know only the **phenomena** (appearances), which are **structured by our mind**.
> - **Space and time** are **forms of intuition**: necessary conditions for perceiving anything. **Categories** such as **causality** and **possibility** structure phenomena at a conceptual level.
> - These structures are in us, but they are **preconditions for the possibility of experience**. Since the input comes from the world, learning about phenomena is still learning about the world, always mediated by an architecture not itself in the world.

> [!card]- Describe Eddington's two tables (*The Nature of the Physical World*, 1927) and the question they raise.
> - **Table 1**, the familiar one: it has extension, is comparatively permanent, coloured, and "above all it is substantial".
> - **Table 2**, the scientific one: "mostly emptiness", with electric charges rushing about whose combined bulk is "less than a billionth of the bulk of the table itself".
> - Question: which table is **real**, the everyday one, the scientific one, or both, and how are they related? It shows the realism debate is about **which things we are committed to the existence of**.

> [!card]- State Mach's radical empiricism and the conception of theories it leads to.
> - Claims about **unobservable reality are metaphysical** and **cannot have a truth-value**: unless you can observe directly with your senses whether something is true, you cannot decide whether it is.
> - Theories are **merely economical descriptions of our sensory experiences**, tools for ordering past experiences and predicting future ones.
> - This is the **instrumentalist** conception: theories are instruments, not descriptions of a hidden reality.

> [!card]- How were the early logical positivists (Carnap, Neurath) more extreme than Mach, and what three commitments did they hold?
> Mach tied **truth** to observability; they tied **meaning** to it as well.
> 1. **Verifiability theory of meaning:** claims about unobservable entities are not verifiable, therefore **meaningless** (neither true nor false).
> 2. A **sharp theory-observation distinction**.
> 3. **Theoretical sentences are introduced through observational sentences.**
> Picture (Hempel's and Feigl's "net"): a network of theoretical terms joined by laws, floating above the plane of observation and anchored to it only by correspondence rules, from which the terms get whatever meaning they have.

> [!card]- Why did the logical positivists need the theory-observation distinction to be sharp?
> Meaning stopped at observability, and **meaning does not come in degrees**: a claim cannot be "almost meaningful". So the boundary between theoretical and observational had to be precise, with no vagueness in it.

> [!card]- What two changes moved the later logical empiricists towards realism?
> 1. **Acceptance of semantics:** general and theoretical sentences can be true or false.
> 2. **Verification replaced by confirmation:** a claim counts as meaningful if there can be confirmation for it, a much broader notion than the stringent demand of verification.

> [!card]- Give Grover Maxwell's (1962) argument against the theory-observation distinction step by step, and say what it threatens.
> 1. Seeing through **glasses** is plainly observation; putting them on does not move you into the theoretical realm.
> 2. Seeing through a **window** is observation too.
> 3. Glasses are not different **in principle** from a **microscope** or telescope: just more complicated arrangements of lenses.
> 4. Yet a microscope image does not seem to count as observation the way looking through glasses does.
> 5. There is **no principled place on the continuum** (with all the intermediate instruments one could build) where observation stops.
> Threat: the empiricist's position depends on a distinction it cannot draw. Tie it to meaning or existence and you cannot say when things are meaningful. Historically an effective argument.

> [!card]- Define scientific realism and empirical adequacy, and say how they differ.
> - **Scientific realism:** empirically well-confirmed theories are **true or approximately true**, including what they say about **unobservables** (fundamental particles, viruses, posits that explain what we observe).
> - **Empirical adequacy:** a theory represents, describes and predicts the **observable phenomena** well; what it says about the observable is true.
> - Realism goes **beyond** empirical adequacy: the unobservable parts of a well-confirmed theory are (approximately) true as well.

> [!card]- State van Fraassen's (1980) formal definition of empirical adequacy and explain each technical term.
> A theory is empirically adequate **iff it has at least one model such that all appearances are isomorphic to empirical substructures of that model.**
> - **Model:** on van Fraassen's **semantic view**, a theory is a family of mathematical structures (models), not a set of sentences.
> - **Empirical substructures:** the parts of a model that are candidates to represent observable phenomena (e.g. relative positions and motions of observable bodies in Newtonian mechanics, leaving out absolute space and forces).
> - **Appearances:** the structures described in measurement and experimental reports.
> - **Isomorphic:** mapped one-to-one, structure-preservingly.
> So the theory "saves the phenomena"; what the rest of the model says about unobservables does not matter for empirical adequacy.

> [!card]- Name the three theses of scientific realism, what each concerns, and what each claims. How do they combine?
> - **Semantic realism** (language): interpret theories **literally**; "there are electrons" means there is a thing with the properties the theory ascribes. Contrast the logical empiricist construal, which paraphrases theoretical terms away as shorthand for observations.
> - **Epistemic realism** (knowledge): we are **justified** in accepting theories as true; empirical success justifies belief, including about unobservables.
> - **Metaphysical realism** (what there is): the theory **is** (approximately) true; its entities exist and its properties are actual.
> They are not alternative definitions: a realist accepts **the conjunction**, and an anti-realist rejects **at least one**.

> [!card]- What is the difference between semantic and metaphysical realism, and what are the two senses of "metaphysical realism" in the realism debate?
> - **Semantic** realism concerns **truth** of sentences: claims about electrons are literally true or false. **Metaphysical** realism concerns **existence**: there actually are electrons. "The theory makes true claims about electrons" and "there are electrons" are different commitments.
> - Two senses: (1) the general thesis that an **external world exists** independently of the mind (against Berkeley); (2) the third component of scientific realism, that **the theory is (approximately) true and its entities exist**. In the realism debate everyone accepts (or brackets) sense 1; the argument is about sense 2.

> [!card]- Classify the scientific realist, Mach and the early positivists, and van Fraassen on the semantic, epistemic and metaphysical theses.
> - **Scientific realist:** yes, yes, yes.
> - **Mach, early logical positivists:** no on all three. Semantic fails because unobservable talk is meaningless or truth-valueless.
> - **Van Fraassen:** **semantic yes**, epistemic no, metaphysical no (agnostic: belief about unobservables is "up to us").

> [!card]- State Putnam's no-miracles argument, give its standard examples, and explain why empirical adequacy alone cannot account for novel success.
> "Realism is the only philosophy that does not make the success of science a **miracle**." If theories were not **truth-tracking**, it is hard to see how they could predict experiments **never performed before**.
> Examples: the **top quark** and the **Higgs boson**, predicted on theoretical grounds before any experiment, both unobservable, and science does this repeatedly.
> Why adequacy is not enough: a theory built to fit past observations is adequate to **those**; nothing about that explains why it gets right things nobody has observed yet. Being consistently right by guessing would be the miracle.

> [!card]- Lay out the no-miracles argument as an inference to the best explanation: explanandum, best explanation, conclusion.
> - **Explanandum:** scientific theories predict successfully the outcomes of experiments never performed before.
> - **Best explanation:** the **approximate truth** of the theory.
> - **Conclusion:** theories that achieve a certain level of success in prediction and experimental testing are (probably) approximately true.
> General IBE: given evidence and rival hypotheses that would each explain it, infer the truth of the one that **best explains** it.

> [!card]- Compare deduction, induction and abduction (IBE) on ampliativity, fallibility and what each appeals to.
> - **Deduction:** not ampliative (the conclusion only takes out what is in the premises), not fallible (truth-preserving), appeals to **logical form**.
> - **Induction:** ampliative, fallible, appeals to **observed frequencies and statistics** (a pattern continues).
> - **Abduction (IBE):** ampliative, fallible, appeals to **explanatory considerations** (this is the best explanation available, so it is true).
> Ampliative: the conclusion goes beyond what is logically contained in the premises.

> [!card]- Why does IBE fail to move many anti-realists, and how does van Fraassen's theory of explanation fit in?
> Anti-realists often **do not accept explanation as an aim of science**. If explanation is not something science aims at, or does not bear on truth, then "it is the best explanation" is no reason to believe it. On van Fraassen's **pragmatic theory of explanation**, explanation is something we do **with** a theory, external to science, adding nothing to what we believe. That makes IBE toothless for him.

> [!card]- Give van Fraassen's own formulations of scientific realism and constructive empiricism, and the two respects in which they differ.
> - **Realism:** "Science aims to give us, in its theories, a literally true story of what the world is like; and acceptance of a scientific theory involves the belief that it is true."
> - **Constructive empiricism:** "Science aims to give us theories which are empirically adequate; and acceptance of a theory involves as belief only that it is empirically adequate."
> Differences: the **aim of science** (literal truth versus empirical adequacy) and **what acceptance commits you to believing** (truth versus only empirical adequacy). Acceptance carries no claim about truth, approximate truth, or truth about unobservables.

> [!card]- Where exactly does van Fraassen disagree with the realist, and on which realist theses does he side with them?
> - The disagreement is about **unobservable entities** only. Everyone in the debate is a metaphysical realist about an external world.
> - **Semantic realist:** claims, including about unobservables, are literally true or false, so meaningful. He refuses the logical positivist route.
> - **Epistemic and metaphysical anti-realist:** well-confirmed theories license belief only in empirical adequacy.
> - Belief about unobservables is **up to us**. He does not say electrons do not exist; he says the evidence does not oblige you to believe they do.

> [!card]- List the four realist arguments van Fraassen attacks in "Arguments Concerning Scientific Realism".
> 1. The **theory-observation distinction** cannot be drawn (§2): Maxwell's argument.
> 2. **Inference to the best explanation** (§3).
> 3. **The demand for explanation** (§4 and §6): the subject of the Musgrave debate.
> 4. **The Ultimate Argument** (§7): the no-miracles argument under the name Putnam gave it.

> [!card]- How does van Fraassen answer the realist's appeal to inference to the best explanation?
> - The realist's claim is a **psychological hypothesis**: that we "follow" the IBE rule, believing the best explanation is **true**.
> - Van Fraassen offers a **rival hypothesis** that fits our practice just as well: we are always willing to believe that the theory which best explains the evidence is **empirically adequate**.
> - So constructive empiricism is a **viable alternative**: inferential practice (and scientists' behaviour) does not force realism.

> [!card]- Why does a vague observable/unobservable boundary refute the logical positivist but leave van Fraassen untouched?
> The positivist made **meaning** (and existence) depend on the line, and meaning and existence are all-or-nothing, so the line had to be sharp. Van Fraassen makes only **epistemic commitment** depend on it. A vague boundary for what we are obliged to believe is harmless: there are clear cases on each side (a table, an electron), and clear cases are all the argument needs. Glasses do not change whether something is observed; a microscope does.

> [!card]- Explain van Fraassen's charge that the theory-observation distinction is a category mistake.
> Two distinctions have been run together:
> - **Language:** theoretical versus non-theoretical **terms**.
> - **Objects:** observable versus unobservable **entities**.
> Terms are bits of language; observability is a property of things. Once separated, meaning no longer depends on observability, and "observable" can be a vague predicate about things.

> [!card]- State the premises and conclusion of Laudan's pessimistic meta-induction ("A Confutation of Convergent Realism", 1981), and say why it is a *meta*-induction.
> 1. Many theories now considered **false** were once **empirically successful**.
> 2. They assumed terms like **"aether"** and **"phlogiston"** referred, as realists now say "electron" refers.
> 3. We now take those terms **not to refer**; the theories were strictly false.
> 4. Induction: presently successful theories may well turn out false too. We are **not justified in believing theories are true**.
> 5. So there is no reason to suppose the entities, properties and processes of our best theories are real.
> **Meta**: the induction runs over **theories**, a higher level than observations. Core thought: we have no reason to think our predicament differs from that of scientists in the 1800s or 1900s, who were just as sure; thinking we finally got it right would be arrogance.

> [!card]- What is Laudan's "historical gambit"? Give at least six of his examples and his summary of what they share.
> His list of once-successful theories: crystalline spheres of ancient and medieval astronomy; humoral medicine; effluvial theory of static electricity; catastrophist geology (universal deluge); phlogiston chemistry; caloric theory of heat; vibratory theory of heat; vital force physiology; electromagnetic aether; optical aether; circular inertia; spontaneous generation.
> Laudan: the list "could be extended *ad nauseam*" and involves "in every case a theory which was once successful and well confirmed, but which contained central terms which (we now believe) were non-referring". What matters is the general inductive point.

> [!card]- What is convergent realism, what are its three elements, and why must reference be preserved?
> Theories are approximately true and successive theories get **closer and closer to the truth**.
> 1. **Approximate truth:** the more mature a theory, the closer to the truth.
> 2. **Reference:** mature theories refer and **preserve reference** across theory change.
> 3. **Empirical success:** new theories explain why old theories were successful.
> Reference must be preserved, otherwise successive theories would be about different things and it would be unclear what they converge on.

> [!card]- How does Laudan attack each link between approximate truth, reference and empirical success?
> - **Approximate truth to reference: "vague".** Approximate truth is too ill-defined to deliver reference.
> - **Approximate truth and empirical success: "no guarantee / false".** Success does not guarantee approximate truth (the historical cases).
> - **Reference and empirical success: "false".** Referring theories can be unsuccessful, and successful theories can fail to refer.

> [!card]- What are Laudan's three related problems for convergent realism, and what ties them together?
> 1. **No good definition of "approximate truth".** Few writers have defined it, and there is no accepted definition. Truth, like meaning, seems not to come in degrees, so the notion is hard to make sense of even formally.
> 2. **No justification for the success-reference link.** The realist's route is success → approximate truth → reference. With approximate truth undefined and doubtful, the link between success and reference is "extremely tenuous".
> 3. **Central terms of once-successful theories do not refer** (aether, phlogiston), so those theories cannot be approximately true, yet they were successful. Success does not indicate approximate truth.
> Common root: **approximate truth**, load-bearing for the realist and extremely hard to make sense of.

> [!card]- Why does the realist need reference to get from success to truth, and what does old tectonic plate theory show?
> A claim is true roughly when its terms **refer** and it says true things about their referents ("my table is made of wood" is true if "table" and "wood" refer and the table really is wooden). So success is the reason to think a theory approximately true, and reference is what that truth requires.
> Old **tectonic plate theory**: "tectonic plates" referred, but the theory was unsuccessful. Reference does not bring success with it.

> [!card]- What are Saatsi's (2005) two readings of the pessimistic meta-induction, and why is the second harder to resist?
> 1. **Induction on the past:** past successful theories were false, so **current theories are likely false**. The strong, first-order reading.
> 2. **Timeless argument against the NMA:** it attacks the realist's argument for optimism by showing **success is not a reliable indicator of truth**.
> The second does not need to predict that today's theories are false. It only needs the record of successful-but-false theories to break the inference from success to truth, which makes the NMA's "miracles" look much less miraculous.

> [!card]- What distinction between ultimate and non-ultimate explanation does Musgrave draw against van Fraassen?
> - **Ultimate** explanations **remove puzzlement**.
> - **Non-ultimate** explanations **relocate and enhance** puzzlement, yet are **real explanations**.
> - There can always be better or deeper explanations; theories do aim to explain, but there is **no unlimited demand** for explanation.
> Musgrave's charge: van Fraassen conflates "realism with essentialism, ... the demand for explanation with the demand for ultimate explanation".
