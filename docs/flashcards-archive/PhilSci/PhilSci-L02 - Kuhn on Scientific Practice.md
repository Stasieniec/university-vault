> [!exam]- Mock exam Q4: Thomas Kuhn compares scientific revolutions, which consist in a change of paradigm, to visual "Gestalt switches", in which "what were ducks in the scientists' world before the revolution are rabbits afterwards". Explain why Kuhn makes this comparison.
> Follow the model answer (two reasons, both needed):
> 1. **A revolution is a radical, sudden shift.** Like a conversion experience, it makes one see the world in a different light all at once, without piecemeal accumulation of new facts. The duck becomes a rabbit as a whole.
> 2. **It expresses his view of observation**, inspired by the Gestalt psychologists: observation is **theory-laden**. What scientists see, and what they take their observations to show, depends on the paradigm they bring to it, so after a revolution the same data are organised into a different whole.
>
> Support with the young woman / old woman priming experiment or the Bruner and Postman playing-cards experiment, and name the consequence: there is no fully theory-neutral observation language to adjudicate between paradigms (incommensurability).
>
> Losing marks: giving only one of the two reasons.

> [!exam]- Mock exam Q5: What does Helen Longino mean by the statement that objective scientific knowledge has a "social character"? How does something become "scientific knowledge"? Give some examples of social mechanisms that secure objectivity.
> Follow the model answer:
> 1. **Social character.** Knowledge becomes scientific through the participation of **two or more individuals**, in a process of **critical emendation and modification** of individual contributions. Each such step is transformative.
> 2. **How something becomes scientific knowledge.** By surviving this process: a claim criticised from several points of view and modified in response is freed from the subjective preferences of any single investigator. Objectivity is a property of the community's practice.
> 3. **Mechanisms:** **replication** of experiments with variations; **peer review**, which brings another point of view on the phenomena so the interpretation is freed from subjective preferences; **treatment after publication** (refining new ideas and techniques); **absorption into knowledge** (subsequent citation, use and modification by others).
>
> For depth: tie it to contextual empiricism (data support a hypothesis only against background assumptions, which may be value-laden, so an individual cannot guarantee objectivity alone) and list the four conditions for transformative criticism: recognised avenues for criticism, shared standards, responsiveness to criticism, equality of intellectual authority.

> [!exam]- Describe Kuhn's model of scientific development, illustrate it with one historical case, and explain why it challenges the idea of cumulative progress.
> - **Cycle:** a shared **paradigm** organises **normal science** (puzzle solving); a **persistent anomaly** leads to **crisis**; in **extraordinary science** rival approaches are explored and standards are contested; a **revolution** installs a new paradigm, which defines new puzzles, and normal science resumes.
> - **Case:** chemistry went from a preparadigmatic stage (alchemy) to the phlogiston paradigm (Priestley: combustion releases phlogiston) to the oxygen paradigm (Lavoisier: combustion is combination with oxygen). The substance, the explanatory vocabulary and the interpretation of weight changes all changed. Alternatively Ptolemy (geocentric) to Copernicus (heliocentric), where the use of "planet" changed.
> - **Against cumulative progress:** a revolution is non-cumulative. Observation is theory-laden, and paradigms differ in meanings (semantic incommensurability) and standards (methodological incommensurability), so no neutral algorithm compares them and it becomes unclear in what sense the new paradigm is objectively better.
> - **Close with Kuhn's reply:** five shared epistemic values keep theory choice rational.

> [!exam]- Kuhn is often accused of relativism and irrationalism. State the criticisms and explain how Kuhn's account of theory choice answers them.
> - **Criticisms:** paradigm victory is an irrational social victory; no clear demarcation criterion; "paradigm" is unclear (Masterman counted 21 uses in SSR); no objective criteria for progress, hence relativism; Lakatos: Kuhn vindicates "mob psychology".
> - **Reply** ("Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice", 1977): five shared criteria, **accuracy, consistency, broad scope, simplicity, fruitfulness**, are "*the* shared basis for theory choice". They are objective in the sense that the scientific community shares them.
> - They are **values, not algorithmic rules**: each is imprecise, and they can conflict (more accurate but less simple), so scientists weigh them differently. Every choice mixes shared and individual criteria, and previous experience counts.
> - Hence **rational disagreement** is possible and legitimate, and it is needed for progress: if everyone switched at once, competing possibilities would not be developed or tested.

> [!exam]- Can science be value-free? Compare the value-free ideal with the positions of Kuhn, Douglas and Longino.
> - **Value-free ideal** (Weber, logical positivists): values may enter agenda setting and application (panels 1 and 3 of the triptych), never scientific research itself (panel 2).
> - **Weak value-free ideal:** non-epistemic values allowed in panels 1 and 3, **epistemic values** allowed in panel 2.
> - **Kuhn:** his five values are epistemic, and they show that internal theory choice is not mechanical.
> - **Douglas:** values have an **indirect** internal role through **inductive risk**: under uncertainty, the different costs of false positives and false negatives set how much evidence is required. Values do not become evidence.
> - **Longino:** values can enter the **background assumptions** that connect data to hypotheses (what is measured, which models, which comparison groups, what counts as explanation). Objectivity is secured socially, by transformative criticism.
> - Douglas and Longino are **internality positions**: non-epistemic values can legitimately play a role inside scientific reasoning.

> [!card]- What question does Kuhn put in place of logical empiricism's normative ideal, and what methodological moral follows? Quote the first sentence of SSR.
> Logical empiricism concentrated on the logical **justification** of scientific knowledge and a theory-neutral observational language. Kuhn asks instead: **how does science actually work, and what do scientists do?** Moral: study real science in its actual historical detail rather than an idealised picture.
> First sentence of *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), Chapter 1 "A Role for History": "History, if viewed as a repository for more than anecdote or chronology, could produce a decisive transformation in the image of science by which we are now possessed."

> [!card]- Name the five connected features of Kuhn's position on science.
> 1. **History matters:** examine actual episodes of scientific change.
> 2. **Science is dynamic:** its structure changes over time instead of accumulating facts under a fixed method.
> 3. **Critique of scientific progress:** progress as steady accumulation of truths is put in question.
> 4. **Observation is theory-laden:** commitments shape what scientists notice, describe and count as evidence, so there is no fully theory-neutral observational language.
> 5. **Science is social:** paradigms, standards, training, criticism and acceptance belong to communities.

> [!card]- Contrast the positivist and the antipositivist (Kuhnian) periodisations of the history of science, as drawn by Galison (1997).
> - **Positivist:** observation and experiment form **one continuous band**; theory is broken into blocks that change as needed to accommodate new data. Continuity comes from the accumulation of empirical results.
> - **Antipositivist (Kuhn):** theory comes first, and theory and observation are **co-periodised**: every powerful change in theory carries a shift in the standards of observation, so the breaks in both rows line up.
> - Point: there is no continuous, theory-neutral observational base underneath theory change.

> [!card]- What is a Kuhnian paradigm? Give its three defining points, its elements, and define an exemplar.
> 1. A framework for practising science, shared by the **scientific community** of a discipline.
> 2. It holds that community together, so science is a **social enterprise**.
> 3. Its elements (an open list): theoretical principles, methods of investigation, standards, tools, exemplars, metaphysical assumptions.
>
> An **exemplar** is a concrete, standard problem-solution that students learn to imitate. A paradigm tells researchers what counts as a legitimate problem, how to investigate it and what a good solution looks like, so it is wider than a theory.

> [!card]- Fill in the Newtonian paradigm: theoretical principles, methods, tools, exemplars, metaphysical assumptions.
> - **Principles:** Newton's laws, e.g. $\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ (vector sum of forces equals mass times acceleration).
> - **Methods:** the *regulae philosophandi* (rules of reasoning in natural philosophy).
> - **Tools:** differential equations and mathematical modelling.
> - **Exemplars:** the Earth-Sun system and the harmonic oscillator (mass on a spring: Hooke's $F = -kx$ in the second law gives $m\ddot{x} = -kx$).
> - **Metaphysical assumptions:** absolute space and time, central to the Newton-Clarke/Leibniz debate.

> [!card]- Define normal science and a puzzle, and list what scientists do in normal science.
> **Normal science:** research within an accepted paradigm whose main activity is solving the puzzles the paradigm defines, without testing whether the paradigm is true.
> **Puzzle:** a problem whose solution is expected in principle under the accepted framework.
> Activities: refining measurements, extending applications, articulating theory, resolving apparent discrepancies.

> [!card]- Why is normal science conservative, why does Kuhn think this is productive, and how does it contrast with Popper?
> The paradigm is accepted unconditionally. Critics are marginalised or dispelled, and a failure reflects on the scientist rather than the paradigm: "only a poor workman blames his tools." So normal science is **conservative** and seemingly **dogmatic**.
> This **allows progress**: because the foundations are not constantly reopened, researchers can work in detail.
> Contrast: Popper's ideal is permanent critical testing of theories.

> [!card]- Popper and Kuhn both deny that astrology is a science. What reason does each give?
> Only the comparison itself is in the course material; the two reasons are the standard reading, not settled by it.
> - **Popper:** astrology is not falsifiable in practice: vague predictions, failures explained away.
> - **Kuhn** (reply to Popper, "Logic of Discovery or Psychology of Research?"): astrology lacks a **puzzle-solving tradition**. Its failed predictions generated no puzzles a community could work on, so there was no normal science.

> [!card]- Draw Kuhn's three-box model of scientific development with the two labels in each box, and say how to read it.
> 1. **Normal science:** paradigm, puzzles.
> 2. **Crisis:** discussion on paradigm, anomalies.
> 3. **Revolution:** new paradigm, puzzles.
>
> Reading: a paradigm defines puzzles; when puzzles turn into anomalies, the paradigm itself becomes a topic of discussion; a revolution installs a new paradigm with a new set of puzzles, and normal science resumes. The fuller cycle adds extraordinary science between crisis and revolution.

> [!card]- Define anomaly, crisis, extraordinary science and scientific revolution. How does an anomaly differ from an ordinary failed experiment?
> - **Anomaly:** a puzzle that persistently resists solution and appears inconsistent with the paradigm's expectations. Ordinary failures are blamed on error, poor technique or incomplete work.
> - **Crisis:** important anomalies undermine confidence in the paradigm; it has social and psychological as well as evidential dimensions.
> - **Extraordinary science:** researchers entertain competing approaches, and even the standards for judging theories are contested.
> - **Revolution:** a non-cumulative change in which a new paradigm replaces the old one, changing central concepts, problems, methods, standards, and sometimes what counts as an observation or explanation.

> [!card]- Give Kuhn's three-stage history of chemistry and explain why he counts the last shift as revolutionary.
> 1. **Preparadigmatic:** alchemy, with no stable community-wide framework.
> 2. **Phlogiston paradigm** (Priestley): combustion is the release of phlogiston from burning bodies.
> 3. **Oxygen paradigm** (Lavoisier): combustion is combination with oxygen.
>
> Revolutionary because the framework changed: the relevant substance, the explanatory vocabulary and the interpretation of weight changes all changed, which is more than adding a new fact.

> [!card]- Describe the Ptolemy-Copernicus shift as a Kuhnian revolution, including its qualifications.
> **Ptolemy:** geocentric world-picture, Earth stationary at the centre. **Copernicus:** heliocentric world-picture, Earth one of the planets moving around the Sun. The shift reorganised astronomical problems and the use of terms such as "planet". It was not an instant replacement and the winner did not erase every rival, but it is Kuhn's paradigm case of revolutionary reorganisation.

> [!card]- How does the "My Wife and My Mother-in-Law" priming experiment work, and what does it show about paradigms?
> Parts of the audience are first shown an unambiguous **young woman** (seen from behind, choker, fur collar) or an unambiguous **old woman** (large nose, chin in collar). Then everyone sees the **ambiguous figure**, where the young woman's jaw and ear are the old woman's nose and eye, and her choker is the old woman's mouth.
> People tend to see the ambiguous figure as the version they saw first, and may not see the other until it is pointed out. The prior image plays the role of a paradigm: the same stimulus is organised into a different whole depending on what the viewer brings to it.

> [!card]- Using the duck-rabbit, state Kuhn's Gestalt argument about observation, and the limit Kuhn himself notes in the analogy.
> - Gestalt psychology: humans observe patterns as wholes, and observation and interpretation are intertwined.
> - In a Gestalt switch one flips between two ways of seeing the same image; in a revolution scientists see the world in a new way.
> - So observation is **theory-laden** (paradigm-dependent): what scientists observe and take it to show is shaped by their conceptual and practical commitments.
> - **Limit:** the experimental subject can switch back and forth and knows the lines stayed the same; the scientist has no external standpoint from which to check that the world stayed the same.
> - This does not make perception arbitrary or mean scientists inhabit different physical worlds. It means no completely theory-neutral description can settle every revolutionary dispute from outside both paradigms.

> [!card]- Describe the Bruner and Postman playing-cards experiment and Kuhn's reading of it.
> Subjects saw playing cards in brief exposures, some anomalous (a **red six of spades**, a **black four of hearts**). At short exposures they identified anomalous cards as normal ones without any sense of trouble; at longer exposures they hesitated and became confused; eventually most saw them correctly, a few never did.
> Kuhn's reading, as a model of discovery: anomaly is at first not perceived at all, because expectation (the paradigm) shapes perception. It is recognised only against that background of expectation, and only with effort.

> [!card]- What is the Müller-Lyer illusion, what point does Kahneman (2011) draw from it, and how does it cut against strong theory-ladenness?
> Two lines of **equal** length: the one ending in arrowheads pointing outwards looks shorter, the one ending in outward fins looks longer.
> Kahneman (*Thinking, Fast and Slow*): the illusion persists even after you have measured the lines and know they are equal, so perception organises the stimulus automatically.
> The other edge: because knowing better does not change what you see, philosophers such as Fodor used it to argue perception is partly insulated from belief, and so against strong theory-ladenness.

> [!card]- Define incommensurability, explain where the term comes from mathematically, and state Kuhn's later "local untranslatability" version.
> **Incommensurability:** two paradigms cannot be fully measured against one another by a common language or fixed set of standards.
> **Origin:** in Greek mathematics, two lengths are commensurable if some unit fits a whole number of times into both. The side (1) and diagonal ($\sqrt{2}$) of a unit square are incommensurable: $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational, so no $p/q$ equals it and no unit measures both. Yet they can still be related and compared, only not by a shared unit, and the same holds for paradigms.
> **Local untranslatability:** some terms cannot be translated without loss, and no translation may preserve all inferential relations. "Local": the failure is confined to a cluster of interdefined terms (such as "mass", "force", "energy"), while most of the two languages stay intertranslatable, leaving room for comparison.

> [!card]- Distinguish semantic from methodological incommensurability with an example of each, and state the problem they create.
> - **Semantic:** meanings change. "Mass" has different theoretical roles in Newtonian and Einsteinian physics; "planet" changes across the Copernican shift.
> - **Methodological:** standards change. What counts as a satisfactory explanation before Newton may differ from the standard after Newton.
>
> Problem of progress: if meanings and standards change, in what sense is a new paradigm objectively better? This is the question Kuhn's critics press.

> [!card]- List the main criticisms of Kuhn, with who made them where a name is attached.
> - Paradigm victory is an **irrational social victory**; Lakatos: Kuhn vindicates "**mob psychology**" by comparing science with political revolutions.
> - **No clear demarcation criterion.**
> - **Unclear, metaphorical language**, above all "paradigm": Margaret Masterman (*The Nature of a Paradigm*, 1970) found **21** uses of the word in SSR.
> - **Relativism:** no objective criteria to judge progress.
> - Kuhn's own *The Copernican Revolution* (1957) stresses more continuity than SSR suggests, and a "winning" paradigm never eradicates all its competitors.

> [!card]- Name and define Kuhn's five values of theory choice, and give the paper they come from.
> "Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice" (given as a lecture, published 1977 in *The Essential Tension*):
> 1. **Accuracy:** agreement with observation within the theory's domain.
> 2. **Consistency:** internally, and with other accepted theories.
> 3. **Broad scope:** consequences beyond the observations the theory was designed for.
> 4. **Simplicity:** bringing order to phenomena.
> 5. **Fruitfulness:** opening new research and discoveries.

> [!card]- Reconstruct Kuhn's argument from the five shared values to rational disagreement.
> 1. Together the five criteria are "*the* shared basis for theory choice".
> 2. They are **individually imprecise**, so scientists apply them differently.
> 3. They **may conflict** (more accurate but less simple, broader in scope but less consistent), so scientists rank them in different hierarchies.
> 4. So scientists committed to the same criteria can make different choices, partly for contextual reasons. Theory choice is **not algorithmic**: experience counts, and every choice mixes shared and individual criteria.
> 5. Therefore **rational disagreement** is possible and legitimate, and it is required for progress.

> [!card]- State Weber's value-free ideal: what is value relevance, what is value freedom, and how do the logical positivists treat value statements?
> Max Weber (1864-1920):
> - **Value relevance:** values may legitimately influence which research questions, priorities and funding problems are selected.
> - **Value freedom:** once a question is selected, researchers must not present their own commitments as empirical conclusions; empirical claims are judged by evidential and methodological standards.
>
> This parallels the fact-value distinction. The logical positivists held that value statements express subjective attitudes and cannot be true or false.

> [!card]- Describe the triptych (externality) model, the question attached to each panel, and the weak value-free ideal.
> - **Panel 1**, research agenda (problems, priorities, funding): values may enter. **Panel 2**, scientific research: value-free. **Panel 3**, application and policy: values may enter.
> - Douglas calls it the **externality model** because values stay outside the middle panel.
> - Questions: for panels 1 and 3, how do we make the right choices? For panel 2, can "pure" science be value-free?
> - **Weak value-free ideal:** panels 1 and 3 may involve non-epistemic values; panel 2 allows **epistemic values**.

> [!card]- Distinguish epistemic from non-epistemic values with examples, and name the two issues that drive the debate since Kuhn.
> - **Epistemic (cognitive):** conducive to the cognitive aims of science: accuracy, consistency, explanatory power, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness, perhaps beauty. Kuhn's five are generally read as epistemic.
> - **Non-epistemic:** justice, social desirability, political preferences.
>
> The two issues: (1) the epistemic versus non-epistemic distinction itself; (2) **inductive risk**.

> [!card]- State Longino's contextual empiricism and illustrate how values can enter scientific reasoning internally.
> Data support a hypothesis only **relative to background assumptions**. Those assumptions can contain non-epistemic commitments, so values can affect what is measured, modelled, compared, interpreted or accepted as explanatory.
> Example: a medical study treats a symptom as irrelevant, chooses a comparison group, or measures only a narrow outcome. These are background assumptions fixing the evidential link between data and hypothesis, and a diverse critical community can challenge them and improve the evidence.
> Longino does not conclude science is subjective: objectivity comes from **transformative criticism**.

> [!card]- What are Longino's four conditions for objectively transformative discourse, and what question do they raise?
> 1. **Recognised avenues for criticism:** established ways to raise challenges.
> 2. **Shared standards** that critics and participants can invoke.
> 3. **Responsiveness to criticism:** criticism gets uptake and can alter beliefs or practices.
> 4. **Equality of intellectual authority:** relevant members contribute as recognised epistemic agents.
>
> These are the normative criteria for objectivity. Open question: is the objectivity of science saved by social epistemology?

> [!card]- Explain Douglas's distinction between direct and indirect roles for values, and define inductive risk.
> - **Direct role:** values determine a decision or course of action; compatible with the externality picture.
> - **Indirect role:** legitimate inside science when evidence is uncertain and the consequences of error affect how much evidence is needed to accept or reject a claim. This is **inductive risk**.
>
> Values do not become evidence. Where false positives and false negatives have different costs, they set the evidential threshold. Relevant in regulatory science, public health, environmental science and risk assessment.

> [!card]- Match each question about values to the position it fits: agenda and application, decision thresholds, background assumptions.
> - Values select research agendas or applications: the **externality / value-free ideal** can allow this.
> - Values set a decision threshold under uncertainty: **Douglas**, indirect internal role through inductive risk.
> - Values enter background assumptions shaping what counts as evidence: **Longino**, contextual empiricism.
>
> Three roles, in short: epistemic values in theory appraisal (Kuhn), non-epistemic values in decisions under uncertainty (Douglas), contextual values in evidential reasoning (Longino).
